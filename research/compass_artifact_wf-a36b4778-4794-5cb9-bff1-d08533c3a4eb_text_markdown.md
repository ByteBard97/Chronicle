# Building a Social Simulation Layer for 1,000 NPCs: A Literature Survey and Implementation Assessment

## TL;DR
- The strongest foundation is the **UCSC Expressive Intelligence Studio lineage** (Talk of the Town → Neighborly → TED/Simulog) for belief-with-provenance and rumor mutation, combined with **cheap network-science models** (Daley–Kendall rumor SIR + Deffuant–Weisbuch bounded-confidence + Bartlett/Griffiths serial-reproduction mutation) for per-tick propagation, and **ACT-R base-level activation** for memory decay/misremembering — all of which are proven implementable in Python at your scale.
- At ~1,000 nodes, the binding constraint is **per-tick cost and legibility, not model sophistication**: shipped postmortems (City of Gangsters, ~1,200 NPCs; RimWorld; CK3) converge on the same lesson — keep social rules to a **small, hand-crafted, obsessively surfaced set** (the City of Gangsters team judged "more than twenty rules… too many"), make actions and their consequences legible, and prefer reversible actions.
- For norms/obligation/face/reciprocity, use **Versu's social-practices model (exclusion logic)** or a lightweight **normative-MAS obligation-with-sanction** scheme rather than full deontic logic; both are implemented and tractable, but the honest assessment is that norm systems are the least "drop-in" of the five areas and need per-game authoring.

## Key Findings
1. **Belief-with-provenance is a solved design problem** — James Ryan's Talk of the Town gives you a directly reusable data model (facets with value, predecessor, evidence list, strength, accuracy) and a nine-type evidence typology, and it was already written in Python.
2. **Rumor mutation** should be modeled as two separable mechanisms: *who hears it* (epidemic/SIR-style Daley–Kendall on your social graph) and *how it changes* (a Bartlett/Griffiths serial-reproduction "belief mutation graph"). Both are O(edges) per tick.
3. **Opinion/attitude drift** (for reputation and factional belief) is cheaply handled by bounded-confidence models (Deffuant–Weisbuch pairwise, Hegselmann–Krause synchronous) — a few floating-point ops per interacting pair.
4. **Memory decay, misremembering, and source confusion** are best grounded in **ACT-R base-level activation** (a closed-form log-decay formula) plus the Generative Agents recency×importance×relevance retrieval score; both are simple arithmetic.
5. **Shipped social sims fail in predictable ways**: illegibility, over-large rule sets, irreversibility, and player inability to form a mental model. The academic postmortems (Robison/Zubek/Horswill; Horswill's MKULTRA) are more valuable to you than any single algorithm.

## Details

### (1) The UCSC / Ryan / Mateas lineage — post-2017 work and the direct predecessors

**James Ryan, Adam Summerville, Michael Mateas, Noah Wardrip-Fruin — "Toward Characters Who Observe, Tell, Misremember, and Lie" (2nd Workshop on Experimental AI in Games, EXAG @ AIIDE 2015; also indexed as AIIDE vol. 11, pp. 56–62).** This is the direct predecessor to the Game AI Pro 3 chapter you already know, and it is the single most important citation for your belief system. *Mechanism:* Each character holds "an ontology of interlinked mental models," one per person or place. Each mental model is a bundle of **belief facets** (one per attribute: name, hair color, occupation, whereabouts, etc.). Each facet is a data structure holding a **Value**, a link to a nested mental model, a **Predecessor** (the previously-held belief — this is your "provenance/history"), an **Evidence** list, a **Strength** (sum of the strengths of supporting evidence), and an **Accuracy** flag. Knowledge changes via a nine-type evidence typology in four categories: origination (reflection, observation, transference, confabulation, **lie**), propagation (statement, eavesdropping), deterioration (**mutation**), and termination (**forgetting**). Mutation is governed by a character's inherited floating-point **memory attribute**, the facet type (whereabouts mutates more readily than a first name), and belief strength (weaker beliefs deteriorate first), using a **hand-authored belief mutation graph** of state-change probabilities. Belief revision is strength-based: a contradicting belief is adopted only when accumulated evidence strength exceeds the incumbent. *Implementability:* Excellent — it is already Python, and the data structures map one-to-one onto your requirement ("beliefs with provenance and strength"). Talk of the Town simulates an American small town day-by-day; per Ryan's site and 2018 dissertation, a full run spans ~200 years (e.g., town established 1839, run to 1979), and by gameplay time "approximately 200 NPCs" populate the city. (The 2015 paper's worked example follows the founder's line "up through the founder's death seventy years later.")

**Ryan's dissertation, "Curating Simulated Storyworlds" (UC Santa Cruz, 2018)** and the framing concept of **story sifting / story recognition** are the theoretical backbone: a simulation emits a huge event chronicle, most of it dull, and a "curationist" layer selects the narratively interesting subsequences. This matters for you because a 1,000-NPC sim will produce far more grudges/rumors/obligations than a player can attend to; you will need a sifter to surface the good ones. The dissertation also catalogs emergent-narrative failure modes (boringness, granularity extremes).

**Max Kreminski, Melanie Dickinson, Noah Wardrip-Fruin — "Felt: A Simple Story Sifter" (ICIDS 2019, LNCS 11869, pp. 267–281).** *Mechanism:* Events are stored as entity-attribute-value facts in an immutable database (the DataScript library); **sifting patterns** are queries over logic variables plus relations that desugar to a subset of Datalog, supporting non-existence tests (`not-join`), named rules, and recursive rules (e.g., transitive causal chains). The immutable DB enables snapshotting, speculative "what-if" rollback, and planning-style search over actions (preconditions + effects, a structure borrowed from Ceptre). In the Diarytown project, four high-school interns "had authored 85 new actions without expert intervention" within a week. *Implementability:* Very high; a Datalog-over-EAV story sifter is straightforward in Python (e.g., pyDatalog or a hand-rolled query layer). Kreminski's **"Why Are We Like This?" (WAWLT, FDG 2020)** shows the same Felt engine (a JavaScript port) driving a playable co-creative game, and demonstrates **character subjectivity** via character-centric sifting patterns (characters reinterpret events according to their values) — directly relevant to per-NPC belief provenance. Related later sifting work: Winnow (AIIDE 2021), Loose Ends (AIIDE 2022).

**Shi Johnson-Bey, Mark J. Nelson, Michael Mateas — "Neighborly: A Sandbox for Simulation-Based Emergent Narrative" (IEEE CoG 2022, pp. 425–432, doi:10.1109/CoG51982.2022.9893631).** *Mechanism:* An explicit, decoupled rational reconstruction of Talk of the Town, published as an open-source Python package (MIT license, Python 3.8–3.12). Entities are built from **YAML archetypes**; a priority queue of Systems updates each timestep; the **relationship model** is a global directed network in which, per the paper, "friendship and romance are modeled as two scalar affinity values ranging from -50 to 50" (adapted from TotT's "charge" and "spark"), carrying searchable **modifier tags** — "Friend, Enemy, Coworker, and Love Interest." **Life events are production rules with preconditions and post-effects** run at intervals, searching for matching characters who may accept or reject participation. *Implementability:* This is arguably your best starting scaffold — it is literally a Python library built for exactly your problem (community-scale social sim of tens-to-hundreds of characters), and its ECS + rules-engine + relationship-network architecture generalizes toward 1,000 nodes. Johnson-Bey's later work (all open source) extends the same ideas: **Minerva** (Python dynasty sim over SQLite), **Drolta** (a Python story-sifting query language over SQLite), and **TDRS** (a Unity/C# trait-driven relationship system) and **Anansi** (Unity social-sim visual-novel framework).

**Ian Horswill & Samuel Hill — "Fast, Declarative, Character Simulation Using Bottom-Up Logic Programming" (AIIDE 2024, vol. 20, pp. 54–64) and Hill & Horswill, "5000 Characters at Video Frame Rate using Declarative Programming" (EXAG @ AIIDE 2024, CEUR-WS Vol-3926).** *Mechanism:* Bottom-up (Datalog-style) logic programming that computes the full extension of each predicate into indexed tables, making unification "simple loads, stores, and equality tests"; state is split into base tables (persistent), derived tables (recomputed each tick via `If()` rules), and update tables (applied at tick end). The language is **TED** (a typed embedded Datalog in C#); **Simulog** is layered on top to manage temporal entities (people, places, events). *Performance (the key datapoint for your scale question):* TED was "2-3 orders of magnitude faster than Prolog … and only 25% slower than C#"; a 2,000-character / 100-location benchmark on an Apple M3 Pro ran a tick in 7.55 ms (compiled TED) vs 6.01 ms (native C#). The EXAG paper reports: "With 5000 NPCs… on an Apple M3 Pro we are able to achieve 60fps or better for 95% of frames… Our average inner-frame time was 13.87ms with a standard deviation of 2.077ms." Crucially, it adds the fallback: "Without these optimizations [native-C# compilation + per-predicate parallelism] the maximum character count at nearly the same performance is 2000 NPCs." *Implementability caveat:* This is the performance ceiling and it is **C#, not Python.** It matters as a proof that declarative social simulation at your scale is real-time-feasible, and as a warning: a naive Python per-tick loop over 1,000 NPCs with rich rules will be orders of magnitude slower, so keep the hot path arithmetic and vectorize (NumPy) where possible. (For calibration, the same authors note the original Python Talk of the Town, population ~400, "required many minutes to simulate a city.")

### (2) Cheap gossip / rumor / belief-revision models from network science

**Daley–Kendall (1964/65) rumor model and the Maki–Thompson variant.** *Mechanism:* An SIR-analogue partitioning the population into **Ignorants (S), Spreaders (I), and Stiflers (R)**. Unlike disease SIR, recovery is *interaction-driven*: a spreader becomes a stifler when it meets someone who already knows the rumor (rate α), while ignorants become spreaders on contact (rate β). A crucial qualitative difference: rumors spread macroscopically for **any** β/α > 0 (no epidemic threshold on homogeneous mixing). Many extensions exist (SIHR adds "hibernators"/forgetting; ISOVR adds mutation/silence). *Implementability:* Trivial and cheap — a per-tick pass over active edges with a couple of random draws; O(active spreader edges). This is exactly the right model for "who currently knows/repeats rumor X" over 1,000 nodes.

**Deffuant–Weisbuch (2000) and Hegselmann–Krause (2002) bounded-confidence opinion dynamics.** *Mechanism:* Each agent holds a continuous opinion in [0,1]. In DW, a random pair interacts; if their opinions differ by less than a confidence bound c, each moves toward the other by a compromise factor μ (0<μ≤0.5): `x_i += μ(x_j − x_i)`. If the gap exceeds c, nothing happens (this is the "bounded confidence" — you won't be swayed by views too far from yours). HK is the synchronous variant (average all neighbors within the bound). Outcomes are consensus, polarization, or fragmentation depending on c; the number of clusters ≈ ⌊1/2c⌋. *Implementability:* Extremely cheap (one subtraction, one comparison, two updates per interacting pair). Perfect for reputation drift, factional attitudes, and how strongly-held opinions resist contrary rumors. Note it models *attitudes/opinions*, not factual belief content — pair it with the Ryan facet model for the latter.

**Bartlett (1932) serial reproduction, formalized by Griffiths & colleagues (Xu & Griffiths 2010; Kalish, Griffiths & Lewandowsky) as a Bayesian Markov chain.** *Mechanism:* Information passed along a chain is reconstructed, not copied; each retelling is biased toward the transmitter's prior/schema, and the distortion compounds. Formally, serial reproduction is a Markov chain over (stimulus, inferred abstraction) that converges toward the shared prior. Empirically, transmission favors negatively-valenced and schema-consistent content. *Implementability:* This is the principled justification for Ryan's hand-authored **belief mutation graph**. Implement rumor content as a small feature vector and, on each hop, resample features toward a schema/prior with a per-feature mutation probability — O(features) per transmission. Together, Daley–Kendall (spread) + a mutation graph (content drift) + optional negativity bias gives you "rumor propagation with mutation" cheaply.

**Cognitive cascades (Rabb, Cowen, de Ruiter, Scheutz — PLOS One 2022)** is worth knowing as the state of the art that explicitly couples an *internal belief model* to network contagion (most epidemic models omit this) — useful if you find pure SIR too mechanical, though heavier per tick.

### (3) Norms, obligation, face, and reciprocity — implemented, not just theorized

**Richard Evans & Emily Short — Versu (IEEE TCIAIG 2014); Evans's "Introducing Exclusion Logic as a Deontic Logic" (2010) and "Using Exclusion Logic to Model Social Practices" (2011).** *Mechanism:* A **social practice** is "a recurring social situation" (a successor to the Schankian script) implemented as a **reactive joint plan** that provides *affordances/suggestions* to autonomous agents but never controls them directly — each agent chooses via utility-based reactive action selection. Norms/obligations are represented in **exclusion logic**, a modal logic without negation or disjunction that has an efficient decision procedure. *Implementability:* Medium. The full Versu stack is lost, but there is a 2023 open-source rational reconstruction, **Praxish** (Proc. AIIDE 2023), of the exclusion-logic layer. The social-practice-as-affordance pattern is very implementable and is the cleanest published model of obligation/norms for characters; exclusion logic's no-negation restriction is what buys tractability.

**Comme il Faut (McCoy et al.) / Prom Week and its successor Ensemble Engine (Samuel et al., FDG 2015).** *Mechanism:* Social state is a database of relationships, networks, status, and history; characters have volitions computed from a large body of "social considerations" (rules — Prom Week shipped on the order of thousands) ranking desired **social exchanges**; a responder accepts/rejects the intent; **trigger rules** then cascade further state changes. Ensemble is the domain-agnostic, open-source (JavaScript) reimplementation. *Implementability:* High for the exchange/consideration pattern; the caveat is authorial burden. Reciprocity and obligation emerge from the rules rather than being first-class.

**Normative multi-agent systems (NorMAS) literature** (Boella, van der Torre, Dignum; deontic obligation/permission/prohibition with monitoring + sanction; online norm-synthesis mechanisms). *Mechanism:* Norms as explicit deontic statements with an addressee, activation/expiration conditions, and rewards/punishments, enforced by a monitor that detects violations and applies sanctions. *Implementability:* The *concept* (obligation with deadline + sanction) is trivially implementable and is what you actually want for "obligations" as a game mechanic; the *full deontic-logic verification* machinery is overkill for a game. Use the obligation-record-with-sanction pattern, not a theorem prover.

**FAtiMA / OCC / EMA appraisal architectures (Dias, Mascarenhas & Paiva — FAtiMA Modular 2014, FAtiMA Toolkit; Gratch & Marsella — EMA).** *Mechanism:* Events are appraised against goals to produce OCC emotions (22 types) that modulate behavior; the FAtiMA Toolkit is open-source and rule-based (logic-programming appraisal rules). *Implementability:* Medium; more than you need if you only want grudges/reputation, but the OCC appraisal-of-others' actions (praiseworthiness, desirability, desirability-for-others) is a clean, principled way to *generate* grudges and gratitude as first-class, decaying relationship modifiers — a good bridge between "face/reciprocity" and your grudge system.

### (4) Memory, forgetting, misremembering — cognitive models games/agents have operationalized

**ACT-R base-level activation (Anderson & Schooler).** *Mechanism:* A memory chunk's activation `B_i = ln(Σ_k t_k^{−d})`, where t_k are the times since each past access and d is a decay parameter (typically 0.3–0.5). This reproduces the empirical power law of forgetting; retrieval probability/latency is a function of activation, and repeated access re-strengthens the trace (traces sum). *Implementability:* Excellent — a closed-form formula, a few operations per memory. For 1,000 NPCs you will approximate (store a running activation and last-access timestamp rather than the full access history); the literature explicitly discusses these approximations and their pitfalls (the standard approximation is non-monotonic in d, so test your parameters). Recent LLM-agent systems (e.g., "Human-Like Remembering and Forgetting in LLM Agents," HAI 2025) show ACT-R decay layered with semantic similarity and stochastic noise — a template for your retrieval.

**Stanford Generative Agents (Park et al., UIST 2023) retrieval score.** *Mechanism:* `score = α_recency·recency + α_importance·importance + α_relevance·relevance` (all α = 1 in the original), with recency an exponential decay (γ≈0.995/hour) on last access, importance an LLM- or heuristic-assigned 1–10, relevance an embedding cosine similarity; reflections periodically synthesize higher-level beliefs written back into the stream. *Implementability:* High if you drop the LLM (assign importance heuristically and relevance by tag overlap rather than embeddings). This is the de-facto-standard modern memory-retrieval design and composes naturally with ACT-R decay. Watch its documented failure modes: reflection can hallucinate patterns, and retrieved top-k should stay small (3–5).

**Source confusion / misattribution** has no single "game algorithm." Operationalize it by making provenance a mutable, decaying field: use Ryan's evidence typology (each belief carries its Source; corrupt or swap the Source field on retrieval failure) combined with the Bartlett mutation mechanism on content.

### (5) Shipped-game and prototype postmortems

**Robison, Viglione, Zubek & Horswill — "AI Design Lessons for Social Modeling at Scale" (AIIDE 2021, vol. 17, pp. 213–219) and Zubek, Horswill, Robison & Viglione — "Social Modeling via Logic Programming in City of Gangsters" (AIIDE 2021, vol. 17, pp. 220–226, doi:10.1609/aiide.v17i1.18912).** City of Gangsters ships "a network of roughly 1200 NPCs" — almost exactly your target — and NPC opinions of the player modulate success. Four hard-won lessons: (1) **legibility is paramount** — social actions and their consequences must be visible and comprehensible; with a thousand agents, ambiguity kills the experience; (2) **actions should be reversible**; (3) **modeled social norms must be a succinct set**; (4) surface them obsessively. In the authors' words: "A handful of rules, deliberately crafted, obsessively surfaced, and thoughtfully explained, offers a wide range of design depth… having more than twenty rules strikes us as too many." They used top-down logic programming and found it well-suited to inference over a 1,000+ character relationship network. *This is the single most relevant citation to your project.*

**Ian Horswill — "Postmortem: MKULTRA, An Experimental AI-Based Game" (AIIDE 2018, vol. 14, pp. 45–51).** An experimental fully AI-driven game built on logic programming; documents the practical pain of debugging emergent character reasoning and the gap between an expressive knowledge representation and a legible player experience.

**Talk of the Town / Bad News (Ryan et al.).** The simulation was rich but the interactive *gameplay layer was never fully realized* (Bad News instead used a live human performer to surface the simulation) — a caution that a deep social model is necessary but not sufficient; you must build the presentation/sifting layer that makes it playable.

**Shipped comparanda you named, characterized:**
- **Dwarf Fortress** (Tarn Adams — "Secret Identities in Dwarf Fortress," AIIDE 2016; "Characterization and Emergent Narrative in Dwarf Fortress," 2019/2021): models rumors, grudges, relationships, and secret identities, with on-the-fly identity replacement in rumors and witness reports, and relationships tracked at name/visual/reputation granularity. Adams openly notes the social layer got less depth than the physical simulation. Per the wiki, NPCs in some cases "don't actually remember the event" — they re-derive reactions when retold — a pragmatic shortcut worth copying.
- **RimWorld**: a **thought/moodlet** system where social memories (e.g., "you killed my mother," ~−80 opinion) are opinion modifiers that decay over fixed durations, with some (deaths) effectively permanent; opinion drives insults, social fights, and romance thresholds. A clean, cheap, shippable model of grudges as decaying scalar modifiers — the pragmatic opposite of Ryan's rich facets.
- **Crusader Kings 3**: schemes, hooks (weak/strong, expiring after ~a decade), secrets, and a scalar opinion system with stacked, sometimes-expiring modifiers. Notable failure modes from patch notes/player reports: AI acting against its stated personality, and (patch 1.4.0) AI interaction spam throttled from ~300 to ~30 interactions per character per century — a direct warning about **per-tick interaction budgets at scale**.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Prototype the spine (Python).** Start from **Neighborly's architecture** (or reimplement its pattern): entity-component NPCs, a global directed relationship network with scalar affinities plus tag modifiers (copy RimWorld's decaying-modifier simplicity for grudges/obligations), and a small production-rule event system. Represent beliefs with **Ryan's facet structure** (value, predecessor, evidence list with source/time/strength, accuracy). This gets you beliefs-with-provenance, grudges, and reputation immediately.

**Stage 2 — Add propagation and memory (the cheap network models).** Layer **Daley–Kendall** spreading over the relationship graph for "who knows rumor X," a **Bartlett/Griffiths mutation graph** (feature-vector resampling toward schema with negativity bias) for content drift, and **Deffuant–Weisbuch** for opinion/reputation convergence. Add **ACT-R base-level activation** (running activation + last-access timestamp) plus the Generative-Agents recency×importance×relevance score for retrieval, forgetting, and misremembering (corrupt the Source field on retrieval failure for source confusion).

**Stage 3 — Norms and sifting.** Add obligations as **explicit records with deadline + sanction** (the NorMAS pattern, not deontic logic) and, if you want richer norm-governed situations, adopt the **Versu social-practice-as-affordance** pattern (use the Praxish reconstruction as a reference). Add a **Datalog-style story sifter** (Felt/Drolta pattern) so the game can surface the interesting grudges/rumors/betrayals to the player.

**Benchmarks / thresholds that change the plan:**
- **If per-tick cost exceeds your frame/step budget** at 1,000 NPCs in Python: stop adding rules, vectorize the propagation/opinion passes with NumPy, move to level-of-detail (fully simulate NPCs near the player, sample the rest stochastically as Neighborly/TotT do), and — as a last resort — port the hot path to a bottom-up logic engine (TED/Simulog proves 2,000 NPCs is real-time-feasible even without its parallel/compiled optimizations, and 5,000 with them — but in C#).
- **If players can't form a mental model of the social state** (the City of Gangsters failure): cut the rule set toward ~20, make every consequential action legible and reversible, and cap per-NPC interactions per tick (the CK3 300→30 lesson).
- **If emergent events are plentiful but boring** (Ryan's critique): invest in the story sifter and importance-weighting, not in more simulation depth.

## Caveats
- **Scale claims are engine-dependent.** The 5,000-characters-at-60fps (13.87 ms average inner-frame, 95% of frames ≥60fps) and 2,000-character/7.55-ms-tick numbers are from **C# (TED/Simulog)**, not Python; treat them as an existence proof of feasibility, not a Python performance promise. The C# team itself notes that without native compilation + per-predicate parallelism, the ceiling drops to 2,000 NPCs. Expect to profile and vectorize.
- **Bounded-confidence and SIR models describe *aggregate* dynamics.** They give believable statistical spread and clustering but not narratively meaningful individual reasons; the Ryan facet model supplies the per-belief provenance the aggregate models lack. Use them together.
- **Norm/obligation systems are the least drop-in of the five areas.** Every implemented system (Versu, CiF, NorMAS) trades expressiveness for either authorial burden or logical restriction; budget real authoring time and keep the rule set small.
- **Some sources are secondary or informal** (wikis, forum patch notes for RimWorld/CK3, dev interviews for Dwarf Fortress). These are reliable for *design behavior* but are not peer-reviewed; the DF and City of Gangsters AIIDE papers are the citable primary sources.
- **The generative-agents reflection mechanism can hallucinate patterns** and is LLM-dependent as published; the non-LLM adaptation recommended here is a simplification, not the validated original.
- **Venue/version details for the newest work** (exact EXAG metadata for the 2015 Ryan paper; the latest Johnson-Bey releases and their archival status) are drawn from authors' own publication pages and repositories; verify against the final PDFs before formal citation.
