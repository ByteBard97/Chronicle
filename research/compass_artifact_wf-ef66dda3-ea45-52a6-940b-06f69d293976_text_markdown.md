# A GM Agent for a Belief/Grudge/Faction Simulation: A Field Survey of Drama Management, AI Directors, Quest Generation, Player Modeling, and LLM-Era Game Masters

## TL;DR
- **The field validates your two-part split.** Every durable system separates *recognition* (reading state to find what matters) from *intervention* (choosing and framing a response), and the ones that feel best (RimWorld, King of Dragon Pass, Left 4 Dead) intervene by *selecting and casting content against real simulation variables* rather than authoring from scratch — exactly your "GM authors around a sifted situation" model. Your provenance-first design (a radiant quest anchored to a real grudge) is the documented fix for template-quest blandness.
- **The single biggest trust failure in the literature is the "invisible decisive variable."** Directors break trust when they secretly stack the world (rubber-banding, wealth-punishment, forced outcomes) in ways players can detect but not see. The doctrine you already hold — expected-randomness, no invisible decisive variables — is precisely the constraint that Valve, Sid Meier's probability work, and the narrative-mediation literature all converge on. Interventions must be legible as *fair given the fiction*.
- **For an LLM GM over a symbolic sim, make the sim the source of truth and give the LLM one-way, validated authority.** 2024–2026 research is unambiguous: pure-LLM narrators drift badly (best models satisfy <14% of narrative commitments; 40–68% fact-conflict rates), while systems that keep state symbolic and use the LLM only to *frame* validated state (Slice of Life, Orchestrated Reality) or gate its writes through a validator (function-calling, TSL automata at ≥96% adherence) hold together.

---

## Key Findings

1. **Drama management decides *which authored beat/plot-point comes next*, on inputs of story-state + player action + an author-specified quality metric.** Its documented failure mode is combinatorial/authorial: the search is intractable at depth, quality metrics are hard to specify, and "authorial leverage" is often low.
2. **Shipped AI directors decide *pacing and pressure*, on a single legible signal** (L4D: per-survivor emotional intensity; RimWorld: colony wealth + time + population). They stay fair by being *reactive to a signal the player can feel*, and they break when that signal becomes gameable (RimWorld wealth) or when they "cheat" visibly.
3. **Procedural quest generators decide *how to fill a quest template's slots*.** The academic thread (Doran & Parberry grammars) gives structure; the shipped record (Daggerfall → Skyrim Radiant) shows the failure mode is *hollowness from lack of provenance* — slots filled by proximity/availability, not by stakes.
4. **Player-modeling directors (PaSSAGE) decide *which content variant fits the inferred player type*.** It helped a subset of players (inexperienced ones who found the game legible) and cost you a whole modeling/annotation layer and the risk of miscategorization.
5. **LLM-era GMs decide *framing and surface text*, and the entire 2024–2026 lesson is that they must not decide *state*.** The winning pattern is exactly yours: symbolic sim = ground truth; LLM reads sifted state and authors within it; a validator prevents prose from silently mutating the world.

## Details

### (1) The drama-management lineage: what each architecture actually decides

**Façade (Mateas & Stern, 2003–2005).** The unit of decision is the *dramatic beat* — "the smallest unit of dramatic action," a short exchange of ~10–100 joint dialog behaviors written in the reactive-planning language ABL. The drama manager sits *above* the believable agents (Grace and Trip) and *sequences beats chosen from a large pool* to shape a global Aristotelian tension arc. **Inputs:** the player's moment-to-moment natural-language/gestural input (parsed by a shallow, forgiving NLU that deliberately accepts false positives), the topics the player raises, and the current story tension value. **What it decides:** which beat to activate next, and which "mix-in" reactions to fire, to push tension toward the desired arc while preserving local responsiveness. **Documented failure modes:** the architecture is enormously authoring-intensive (three years for a one-act drama, 27 beats); the NLU's permissiveness produces misreadings; and the whole approach does not generalize — the authors themselves note Façade "has never been followed by another similar piece." The core lesson for you: beats give you *authored intervention units that intermix*, but hand-authoring them at fidelity is the bottleneck.

**Declarative Optimization-Based Drama Management / DODM (Nelson, Mateas, Roberts, Isbell, 2005–2008).** Here the decision is *whether and when to fire a "DM action"* (a hint, a reminder, a causing/denying of a plot point) to steer a story made of **plot points with ordering constraints**. **Inputs:** the sequence of plot points the player has triggered so far, plus an **author-specified evaluation function** scoring desired qualities — the literature names four recurring targets: pacing, coherence, player autonomy, and dramatic-arc adherence. The author "declaratively specifies desired properties of the experience; the DM intervenes in a way that optimizes the specified metric." **Two implementations:** (a) online *search* (Weyhrauch's original adversary-search idea, revived), and (b) *reinforcement learning* to precompute a policy offline (Nelson et al. 2006), plus TTD-MDPs (targeted trajectory distribution MDPs) that target a *distribution* of story trajectories rather than a single optimum. **Documented failure modes:** full-depth search is intractable, so early systems used shallow sampling that worked on the toy story "Tea for Three" but *failed to generalize* to the larger interactive fiction *Anchorhead*; and a broader finding — the "authorial leverage" studies (Nelson & Mateas) show it is often hard to demonstrate that DODM actually improves the experience relative to the authoring cost. Notably, DODM training used a **pseudo-adversarial player** (train against a player trying to break the DM), which improved robustness even against random players.

**Riedl's narrative planning and intent-driven agents.** The decision unit is a *plan of character actions*. The **IPOCL planner** (Intent-based Partial-Order Causal Link; Riedl & Young, AAMAS 2004, JAIR 2010) decides a sequence of character actions that is simultaneously *plot-coherent* (audience perceives actions as relevant to the outcome) and *character-believable* (each action is explained by a character goal/intention). This is the crucial contribution for a belief/grudge sim: IPOCL doesn't just find a causally valid plan, it "reasons about character intentionality by identifying possible character goals that explain their actions" — i.e., it manufactures the *provenance* you want. **Experience management / "AI Game Master"** (Riedl, Stern, Thue's "delayed authoring"): the manager monitors player actions and "dynamically modifies the story in a way that the story designer would have wanted," framed computationally as optimizing player experience against designer objectives. **Narrative mediation** (Riedl, Saretto & Young 2003; Harris & Young's proactive mediation 2009) is the key mechanism and the key hazard: when a player takes an "exceptional action" that threatens the plan, the manager either **intervenes** (causes the action to fail / substitutes effects — e.g., Mimesis "surreptitiously substituting an alternate set of action effects") or **accommodates** (re-plans around it). **Documented failure mode — directly relevant to your doctrine:** *intervention is the invisible-decisive-variable failure in its purest research form.* Making the player's action silently fail to preserve the author's plan is exactly the "secretly stacking the world" move that reads as cheating; the later literature (Robertson & Young's "Finding Schrödinger's Gun") explicitly studies the **invisibility/believability** of these interventions and finds that revising the *world's* possibilities is more believable than removing or overriding the *player's* choices. Accommodation (re-plan around the player) is the trust-preserving alternative.

**Where the thread nets out:** a modular RL decomposition (Rowe, Mott & Lester 2014) and surveys (Roberts & Isbell 2008; Thue & Bulitko's "unified understanding of experience management" 2018) frame *all* of these as one problem: an agent that observes an interactive experience and picks interventions to satisfy author goals *without reducing player agency*. Your story-sifter + GM is a clean instance of this framing.

### (2) Shipped AI directors: input signal → intervention vocabulary → legibility

**Left 4 Dead's AI Director (Michael Booth, Valve, GDC 2009).** **Input signal:** each survivor's estimated **"emotional intensity"** (a per-player stress scalar) that rises with damage taken, being grabbed, proximity to death, and killing nearby infected, and **decays over time**. **Recognition:** a simple state machine over aggregate intensity — Build-Up → Sustain Peak → Peak Fade → Relax (~30–45s of calm). **Intervention vocabulary:** spawn/withhold the wandering horde; place special infected (Boomer, Smoker, Hunter); schedule boss infected (Tank, Witch); and *distribute health, ammo, and weapons* along the computed escape path on the navigation mesh. **Stated goals:** "Promote Replayability" and "Generate Dramatic Game Pacing" via "Structured Unpredictability" — population functions "not purely random, nor deterministically uniform." **How it stays fair:** it is reactive to a signal the player *feels* (you know when you've been hammered, and you get respite), and — critically for your doctrine — Valve documents where it *deliberately does not exploit hidden power*: the survivor bots "cannot deal friendly-fire damage," "never use Molotovs," and are teleported back only "when no human is looking." The design principle is explicit: co-op requires "implicit trust in teammate's action choices," so the system constrains itself to *guarantee some undesirable events cannot occur*. This is your "expected-randomness / no-invisible-decisive-variables" doctrine, shipped.

**RimWorld's storytellers (Cassandra Classic, Phoebe Chillax, Randy Random).** **Input signal:** a **"storyteller wealth"** estimate (colony wealth + building wealth + item wealth + colonist/animal count), plus time and a set of secondary flags (recent colonist death/injury, time since last major event). **Recognition/pacing model:** each storyteller is a different distribution over event timing and difficulty. Per the RimWorld Wiki's Cassandra Classic page, "the single raider will appear on Day 5 hour 15 unless a different minor event appears at that exact time," and "Cassandra averages about 8.5 major threats sent per Rimworld year"; Phoebe stretches the gaps (roughly 8 off-days then 8 on-days), and Randy re-rolls freely (an event roughly every 1.35 days, raid-or-not random, with a guaranteed raid by day 13). Raid *points* are computed from wealth then spent to "buy" raiders at fixed combat-power costs: per the Wiki's Wealth page, "1 raid point = 1 combat point. For example, a scyther is worth 150 combat points. The weakest possible raider is worth 30 combat points." **Intervention vocabulary:** raids, manhunter packs, cargo pods, disease, wanderers/trade caravans, "fortune" events. **Why it works (community analysis):** the storytellers function as *procedural authors of genre* — Cassandra = escalating tragedy, Phoebe = builder's calm, Randy = anarchic apophenia — and losing is reframed as narrative payoff ("Losing is Fun"). **How it gets gamed / failure mode — your invisible-variable hazard:** the wealth signal is *hidden but inferable*, so players discover "wealth management" cheese (hide items underground/unstockpiled, burn wealth, avoid carpet/high-value tiles) to suppress raid strength — then complain the game "punishes you for being effective in your trades" and for playing well. The developer defense is telling: wealth scaling "exists to prevent the game from being too easy," not to be an anti-exploit — but because the decisive variable is invisible, a vocal segment reads it as unfair. Direct lesson for your faction director: if a hidden scalar (grudge intensity, faction anger) silently determines the size of the response, sophisticated players will reverse-engineer and game it, and less-sophisticated ones will feel cheated. **Legibility of the *causal anchor* is your defense** — a faction reprisal justified by a belief the player can inspect is fair in a way a wealth-scaled raid is not.

**Shadows of Doubt (ColePowered Games) — the case generator.** A ~1000-NPC city is simulated in real time (jobs, homes, routines, relationships); the "director" **selects a killer archetype** (e.g., "The Ritual Reaper," "Make Way for the New Generation"), each with a *behavioral pattern, signature weapon, and calling-card evidence*, and the murder then *runs in the simulation* so the trail (who was where, who had motive/means) is a real emergent artifact you deductively reconstruct. **Input:** the simulation's own social graph and schedules. **Intervention vocabulary:** spawn a murder of a given archetype; there is exactly one active killer at a time who keeps killing until caught. **Legibility/fairness:** the case is *solvable because the evidence is grounded in real simulation state* — the strongest possible version of your "provenance" thesis. **Documented failure mode:** *repetition/thinness* — reviewers and players find the ~5 archetypes become "highly repetitive" and some cases surface unsolvable/bugged evidence, and the one *hand-crafted* case ("Dead of Night") is widely called far better than the generated ones. Lesson: provenance makes cases *fair and legible*, but a small archetype vocabulary still reads as formulaic; you need breadth of *situation types* your sifter can recognize, not just depth on a few.

**Wildermyth's event selection (Worldwalker Games; Nate Austin's "Procgen in Wildermyth" talk).** **Input/recognition:** "when the player is in a situation that calls for a story, the game searches the database of events for all the stories that could *fit the situation*, and then picks one randomly," with per-situation storytelling constraints and *casting* of the party's real characters (with their generated personality hooks, relationships, and history) into event roles. **Intervention vocabulary:** narrative vignettes that permanently alter characters (transformations, maimings, romances, rivalries, deaths). **Why it works:** because events are *cast with characters the player already owns and cares about*, the output "feels like it was written by real human beings" and the characters feel "yours." **Documented failure mode:** *seam visibility under repetition* — playing several campaigns back-to-back, reviewers "start seeing the patterns in how the game decided which events take place," and characters start to feel like "a box-of-attributes fed into an algorithm." Branching is also "pretty awkward," so most events resolve on a single choice. Lesson: casting real entities into slots is the core of your fix, but the *selection logic itself becomes legible* with exposure — vary triggers and gate on richer state to hide the seams.

**King of Dragon Pass / Six Ages (A Sharp, David Dunham) — scene selection.** This is the **storylet** ancestor (Failbetter's *Fallen London* explicitly credits it). **Recognition:** the narrative is broken into "scenes" (= storylets), each with **prerequisite conditions in square brackets** and **assignable roles** ("a neighbor, a rival, an ally," or a specific clan-ring character); the engine picks eligible scenes, "with conditions to make sure they're relevant or interesting," then **casts real entities into the roles** and favors leaders the player already knows. **Input signal:** the political-economic sim state (herds, wealth, military, magic, curses, diplomacy) plus season and prior choices. **Intervention vocabulary:** ~575–600 scenes, tagged helpful/harmful/grey; the game "doesn't force or prevent" outcomes so much as offer weighted, contextual choices. **How it stays legible/fair:** Dunham's explicit design avoids *branch* combinatorial explosion by giving choices a **consistent political-economic framework** so outcomes read as consequences of the world, not authorial whim; scenes are written to "work in any situation." Six Ages improved on KoDP by **categorizing/tagging scenes** instead of hard-coding special-case lists (the KoDP code had literal `switch` statements enumerating dispute scenes) — a maintainability lesson for your sifter's taxonomy. This is the closest shipped analog to what you're building: *condition-gated, entity-cast storylets over a systemic sim.*

### (3) Procedural quest generation: the academic thread and the shipped record

**Doran & Parberry (2010–2011).** In "A Prototype Quest Generator Based on a Structural Analysis of Quests from Four MMORPGs" (PCGames '11), they analyzed **over 750 quests** (drawn from a corpus of over 3,000 examples) across **World of Warcraft, EverQuest, Vanguard: Saga of Heroes, and EVE Online**, and found quests share a common structure captured by a **generative grammar**: quests reduce to exactly **nine NPC motivations** (e.g., Knowledge, Comfort, Reputation, Serenity, Protection, Conquest, Wealth, Ability, Equipment), each expanded by **2–7 specific strategies** into sequences of **1 to 6 atomic actions** (terminal symbols like `goto`, `get`, `give`, `kill`, `report`, `use`). **What it decides:** given a motivation, recursively expand to a concrete action tree instantiated with real world entities. This grammar was later extended (Witcher-3 analysis added `<give>`/`<report>`; Questgram mixed-initiative tool; CONAN/Conan Exiles generators). **The crucial finding for you:** the value of PCG quests is that they "fundamentally change the game state" and are "concretely instantiated by the assignment of real, in-game entities" — the academic thread already says provenance is the point; the shipped implementations mostly failed to honor it.

**Daggerfall's quest system (the Radiant Story ancestor), fully documented by Daggerfall Unity.** Daggerfall (1996) used a **template-based quest system**; because Bethesda never shipped tools, Donald Tipton's **Template v1.11** compiler/decompiler format became the de-facto standard, and **Daggerfall Unity (Interkarma, open-source, 1.0 in Dec 2023) adopts Tipton's source files directly as its quest data**, giving us a complete, readable spec. **Structure (from the Daggerfall Workshop "Questing" dev blogs and the `QuestMachine`/`Quest` source):** a quest is a text template of **Resources** (`Person`, `Place`, `Item`, `Foe`, `Clock`) plus **Tasks** made of **Conditions** (fire when true) and **Actions** (perform in world: `start timer`, `place foe … at …`, `give pc …`, `teleport pc`, `say <messageID>`). Resources are the "bridge between quest system and the game world," and **the same template is instantiated repeatedly with different bindings** ("same fetch quest from two different mage guildhalls"). Almost all of Daggerfall's high-level gameplay (even Lysandus's ghost screaming "VENGEANCE!") is quest-scripted. **This is the direct ancestor of Skyrim's Radiant system**, which fills slots (location, enemy type, reward, target) at trigger time — some randomized, some not.

**Why template quests feel hollow, and the attempted fixes.** Skyrim's Radiant is widely praised for *seamlessness* (players "can't tell the difference" from hand-crafted) but criticized as **"on the bland side — not nearly as colorful and interesting"** and, at the extreme, as an anticlimax: after the Dark Brotherhood's epic finale you're "asked to go back to petty murder immediately… it almost feels like a demotion." Todd Howard's own framing is that Radiant exists so Bethesda can "control" the open-world experience for players it "goes poorly for" — i.e., it is a *content-availability* system, not a *stakes* system, and Bethesda "stopped short of using radiant story for major quests" because "algorithms can't come up with emotionally-fulfilling stories." Players diagnose the hollowness precisely: the targets/locations "so-called random… revert to old ones," so it's really a *conditional* quest with shuffled bindings. **The root cause is exactly the one your design targets:** the slots are filled by *proximity and availability* (send player to an undiscovered dungeon), not by *provenance and stakes* (send player because of a real grudge). Attempted fixes in the wild are all "add provenance": tie generation to a persistent NPC relationship, to faction state, to a remembered prior action. Your architecture — sift a real grudge, then have the GM cast a quest template around it — is the field's prescribed remedy stated as a system.

### (4) Player modeling for directors: PaSSAGE and successors

**PaSSAGE (Thue, Bulitko, Spetch, Wasylishen, AIIDE 2007–2008), built in Neverwinter Nights.** **What it decides:** which of several pre-authored "encounter" variants to present next, chosen to match the player's inferred style. **The model:** the player is a **vector over five stereotypes derived from Robin Laws' RPG player types** — Fighter, Method Actor, Storyteller, Tactician, Power Gamer — and each dimension is nudged up/down by *predefined annotations on player actions* (an aggressive choice raises Fighter, etc.). This is "preference-informed **delayed authoring**": authors tag which player types would enjoy each event; PaSSAGE picks accordingly. **How much it helped / what it cost.** A user study (101 participants) found that adaptive stories **increased enjoyment for a *subset* of players — those with minimal prior gaming experience who found the game easy to follow** — and did not uniformly beat two fixed, hand-authored stories. **The cost:** you must build and tune a player-model layer, annotate every content unit with player-type affinities, and accept miscategorization risk (a mis-inferred vector selects worse content). Successors: Ramírez & Bulitko (2014) fused PaSSAGE-style modeling with automated planning and a reward function to *select the generated narrative that maximizes fit*; later work reframed it as changing an MDP (Thue & Bulitko 2012) and as BDI player models over *Anchorhead*. **Takeaway for you:** player modeling is a *multiplier* on a director you already have, with real but *segment-limited* payoff; it is not a foundation. Given your provenance-first goal, spend first on the sifter/GM anchoring and treat player-type adaptation as an optional later layer — and if you add it, prefer *observable behavior* over a hidden inferred vector, to stay inside your no-invisible-variable doctrine.

### (5) LLM-era GM agents and the symbolic-truth pattern

**DeepMind Concordia (Vezhnevets, Leibo et al., 2023; v2.0 2025) — the Game Master pattern.** Concordia borrows the **tabletop GM** directly: a special agent "is responsible for simulating the environment where the agents interact." Agents "take actions by describing what they want to do in natural language. The GM then translates their actions into appropriate implementations," **checking physical/social plausibility** and producing **"event statements" that become the observable facts of the world** — the GM is literally "the environment's state transition function." v2.0 uses an **Entity-Component architecture** (GMs are now entities; components like `EventResolution`, `MakeObservation`, `Terminate`, `NextActing` are composable), which is a clean engineering template for your GM layer: recognition/intervention as swappable components over a shared world. Concordia's GM is the reference pattern for "a narrator that resolves intents into grounded state" — but note it is a *simulation/research* tool, and by default *the GM itself owns state*, which is the opposite of your "sim is source of truth" choice.

**AI Dungeon's lessons.** AI Dungeon is the canonical demonstration of what happens with **no symbolic ground truth**: it "forgets the rules, ignores your inventory," lets a level-1 fighter dual-wield or cast unlearned spells, and — the deep lesson — **"you can always just confabulate a hidden context"**, so precedents established earlier are silently violated. The community's diagnosis is that **consistency, not creativity, is the hard problem**: "Get caught bending the rules once, and the player trusts nothing." Bolt-on fixes (a "memory" system, lorebooks, scripting, and finetunes like the Llama-3.3-70B "Nova") reduce but do not eliminate drift. This is the empirical case *for* your architecture.

**Hidden Door (Hilary Mason & Matt Brandwein).** Positioned as "AI Dungeon with a real game master": the LLM narrates *within a structured literary world* (Oz, Wonderland, community worlds) so it must keep choices "consistent with [the world's] internal rules," presenting scenes as illustrated graphic-novel panels and reconciling multiple players' improvised actions. A design review at launch (Ian Bicking, 2025) still found **basic narrative-consistency failures** (the scene "flits back and forth" between locations) and recommends principles that echo your doctrine: **"Commit to outcomes before prose"** — decide the state change first, then let the model write it — and use hand-authored arcs as "productive scaffolding." This is independent confirmation that *state must be decided by a non-LLM layer and then narrated*.

**2024–2026 research on LLM directors over symbolic simulations (the core of your architecture).**

- **Why the symbolic-truth layer is necessary — the negative evidence.** *"Can LLM Agents Stick to the Script?"* (Ma et al., NCP-Bench, arXiv 2608.08160, 2026) argues interactive narrative "is fundamentally a **long-horizon constraint satisfaction problem**" where the plot is "a rigid set of logic constraints — world-state facts, causal dependencies, and mandatory event sequences." Their benchmark casts commitments as **Invariant / Ordering / Achievement** constraints and stress-tests narrators with an **adversarial player** ("I already took the key yesterday," "skip to the ending where the villain is arrested"). Results are stark; in the paper's own words: **"GPT-5.2 has the highest average interaction length (32.92 turns), but its survival rate is only 42% after 20 turns. Fact conflicts are the most frequent failure mode, occurring in 40%–68% of runs across models."** Across all runs the best models satisfy fewer than 14% of narrative commitments, and only ~3.5% of interactions reach 100 turns without an explicit conflict. Root cause named: "LLMs lack explicit state-tracking mechanisms; they must implicitly reconstruct world state from the dialogue history at each turn, leading to drift," and they "yield rather than maintain established facts" under adversarial input. *(Caveat: the paper's auditor is itself LLM-based, audited with Gemini-2.5-Flash and human spot-checks; and it cites near-future model names consistent with its 2026 date.)*

- **The pattern you're building, done rigorously — validated writes.** *"Orchestrated Reality: From Role-Play to Living, Playable Game Worlds"* (Huang, Li, Fang; arXiv 2606.16014; CHI PLAY Companion '26; ref. impl. *WorldLines*) formalizes an LLM game world as a **Parameterized-Action POMDP** where **state is "a tree of canonical JSON entities" owned by a single GM-like orchestration agent**, and the LLM **observes only a narrative projection of state** and *proposes* changes through a **Plan–Diff–Validate–Apply pipeline** that "commits schema-validated, content-hashed JSON deltas." Its diagnosis of current systems is precisely your doctrine: they suffer "**unvalidated writes**: the model can assert any world change, so state silently drifts and accumulates contradictions," whereas a validation guard (schema ∧ permission ∧ rule check) "**prevents prose from silently mutating state**," and "the world owns the world" becomes "a checked invariant rather than a prompt request." Memory is "injected, not recalled" — knowledge is "a deterministic function of committed world state." *(Caveat: explicitly work-in-progress; no effect sizes; even its own authors note un-schematized details still drift — an NPC's missing finger "swapping between left and right hand across a 24-turn session.")*

- **Formal-methods control over the LLM — guaranteed adherence.** *"Procedural Adherence and Interpretability Through Neuro-Symbolic Generative Agents"* (Rothkopf, Zeng & Santolucito, Barnard/Columbia; arXiv 2402.16905; AAAI 2025) synthesizes a **correct-by-construction automaton from Temporal Stream Logic** that decides *which prompt modifier* the LLM gets each turn, so long-horizon constraints (e.g., "cannot go to the cave until you've been to the market," `¬inCave W inMarket`) are enforced by the automaton, not the model. The automaton "serves as the memory of the generative agent rather than the LLM itself." In the paper's words: **"over all of the tasks, our approach using TSL achieves at least 96% adherence, whereas the pure LLM-based approach demonstrates as low as 14.67% adherence"** (across 75 games × 20 turns; on a counting-constrained task, 98.67% vs. 14.67%), and the neuro-symbolic agent made **zero arithmetic errors** vs. 33.67% for the pure LLM.

- **Constrained content generation — function calling as a validity gate.** Gallotta, Liapis & Yannakakis, *"Consistent Game Content Creation via Function Calling for LLMs"* (IEEE CoG 2024; system *LLMaker*) has the LLM emit **function calls into a constraint-enforcing back-end** rather than free-form artifacts, so there is "**no risk of illegal changes**." In evaluation, **function calling never produced an invalid (parser/domain-failing) level**, while baseline prompting "never complete[d] a test case without failing," and it was significantly faster in 4 of 5 cases. This is the mechanism for letting your GM *act* on the sim (spawn a quest, trigger a faction move) without ever writing an illegal world state.

- **Symbolic sim as ground truth, LLM as one-way narrator — the cleanest analog to your design.** Treanor, Samuel & Nelson, *"Slice of Life"* (FDG 2024/2025), pairs the **Ensemble/ESP social simulation** (a deterministic, symbolically represented "social record"; character actions chosen by summing weighted first-order-logic social considerations) with an LLM used **only for surface-text realization**. Their stated principle is verbatim your architecture: "**keeping the simulation state deterministic and free from influence by the LLM in order to maintain authorial control**," and "**the dialogue does not feed back into the symbolic simulation state**." By rendering retrieved symbolic facts to English as prompt context (a form of RAG over symbolic facts), it "avoids… hallucinations, allowing player-driven narratives to better reflect the true state of the simulation." Their cautionary finding: an over-engineered prompt that granted the LLM more latitude *reintroduced* hallucinations (a character "invented that it is Tuesday"). *(Caveat: preliminary 3-config case study, no benchmark numbers.)*

- **Adjacent confirmations.** *LIGS* (CHI EA 2024) feeds NPCs "spatial and event information… maintaining narrative consistency"; MDPI's *Game Knowledge Management System* (2026) uses a "schema-governed LLM pipeline" because "rule-based systems struggle to scale narrative complexity, whereas… LLM-based methods often produce outputs that are structurally invalid"; and the broader survey work (Jørgensen et al. 2025 static-vs-agentic GMs; Yin et al. 2024 on player trust under LLM NPCs) reports immersion gains from agentic orchestration but "treat the world's underlying state as implicit" — the exact gap your validated-state design fills.

| **Daggerfall/Radiant** | Trigger conditions | Template with resource slots | `place foe`, `give pc`, `say`, timers | *Slots filled by availability, not stakes → hollow* |
| **PaSSAGE** | Annotated player actions | 5-D Laws player-type vector | Select encounter variant by type | Helped inexperienced players; hidden model is a risk |
| **Slice of Life / Orchestrated Reality** | Symbolic sim state | Deterministic rules / validated deltas | LLM frames validated state; validator gates writes | Grounded, hallucination-resistant; state stays symbolic |

### Which components transfer to a director over a belief/grudge/faction sim

1. **Storylet casting (KoDP/Six Ages, Wildermyth) is your highest-value transfer.** Your sifter produces a *situation with real entities* (a grudge between A and B, a belief C holds); the GM should treat interventions as **prerequisite-gated storylets with role slots** and cast the *actual* grudge-holder, target, and faction into them. This is the mechanism that makes generated content feel authored, and it directly fixes Radiant hollowness because the roles carry provenance.
2. **Intent/provenance reasoning (IPOCL) is the theory behind your anchoring.** IPOCL exists precisely to make character actions *explained by character goals*. Your belief/grudge state *is* the goal structure; the GM should surface the causal "because" (a faction moves *because* of an inspectable belief) so the intervention reads as motivated, not arbitrary.
3. **A single legible pressure signal with self-limits (L4D).** For pacing your dramatic events, use a felt, decaying tension signal and *publish the self-limits* (what the director will never do), the way Valve guarantees bots can't friendly-fire. This is how you honor expected-randomness.
4. **Validated, one-way LLM authority (Slice of Life / Orchestrated Reality / function-calling).** If the GM is an LLM, it *reads sifted state and authors within it* but **writes only through a validator** (schema ∧ permission ∧ rule), never mutating belief/grudge/faction state as a side effect of prose. This is empirically the difference between coherence and drift.
5. **Optional player-type adaptation (PaSSAGE) as a late multiplier**, kept observable rather than hidden.

Skip or de-prioritize: full narrative *mediation-by-intervention* (silently failing player actions), DODM-style global optimization (authorial-leverage payoff is unproven and authoring-heavy), and GM-owns-state designs (Concordia default) that contradict your source-of-truth choice.

### The documented ways directors break player trust
1. **Invisible decisive variables.** RimWorld's hidden wealth scalar and any secret "stack the deck" reads as unfair once inferred — the field's central trust failure and the one your doctrine names.
2. **Overriding the player's action (mediation-by-intervention).** Silently failing or re-effecting a player's choice to protect the author's plan is the research-purest form of cheating; Robertson & Young show *revising the world's possibilities* is more believable than removing/overriding the player's choice.
3. **Rubber-banding / fudged odds mis-tuned.** In his GDC 2010 keynote and his book *Sid Meier's Memoir!*, Meier recounts that in Civilization "it didn't matter how many different ways this conversation played out, I couldn't convince our testers that it made sense for them to lose a three-to-one battle roughly one-fourth of the time. Past certain odds, people expected to win no matter what" — so in Civ Revolution he "tweaked the math to make the player win more." Conversely, Civ 3's *fair* fixed random seed made players feel cheated when a reloaded combat replayed identically. Perceived fairness ≠ statistical fairness; a director that manipulates outcomes must respect player *expectations*, not just correct probability.
4. **Repetition exposing the machine.** Shadows of Doubt's few archetypes, Wildermyth's visible selection patterns, and Radiant's reverting targets all convert "authored" into "formulaic" under exposure.
5. **State drift / broken precedent (LLM GMs).** AI Dungeon's confabulated hidden context and NCP-Bench's 40–68% fact-conflict rates: once the world contradicts itself, "the player trusts nothing."
6. **Hollowness from missing stakes.** Radiant quests feel like "a demotion" because the slot-filling has no provenance — the failure your whole design exists to prevent.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the intervention layer as prerequisite-gated, entity-cast storylets (no LLM required yet).** Model each GM intervention as a KoDP-style storylet: a condition over sifted state (a grudge above threshold, a belief held by a faction leader), a set of **role slots**, and an **intervention action vocabulary** (spawn quest, trigger faction move, alter rumor, escalate grudge). Cast *real* sifted entities into roles so every output carries provenance. Tag storylets (helpful/harmful/grey, genre) as Six Ages did, and drive selection from an explicit taxonomy your sifter emits. **Benchmark to advance:** playtesters can, unprompted, explain *why* an event happened ("the Companions came after me because I sided with the Silver Hand"). If they can't, your anchoring isn't surfacing.

**Stage 2 — Add a legible pacing signal with published self-limits.** Introduce one felt, decaying "dramatic pressure" scalar to space major events (L4D-style), and *document to the player, in-fiction, what the director will never do* (e.g., a faction never gains members it didn't recruit on-screen; a grudge never spawns a reprisal larger than the grudge justifies). **Threshold that changes the plan:** if players report "the world feels like it's out to get me" or discover a suppression exploit (RimWorld wealth-cheese equivalent), your decisive variable is too hidden — move the causal anchor into inspectable state (a visible grudge/belief the reprisal cites).

**Stage 3 — Introduce the LLM GM as a one-way, validated author.** Let the LLM *read sifted state and write surface content* (quest text, dialogue, faction proclamations) but **never mutate belief/grudge/faction state except through a validator** (schema ∧ permission ∧ rule), following Slice of Life ("free from influence by the LLM," "does not feed back into the symbolic state") and Orchestrated Reality's Plan–Diff–Validate–Apply. Use **function-calling** so the GM's *actions* on the sim are structurally incapable of illegal writes. Adopt Hidden Door's "**commit to outcomes before prose**": the symbolic layer decides the state change; the LLM only narrates it. **Benchmark:** run an NCP-Bench-style adversarial-player stress test on your own content — count fact-conflicts and commitment violations per 20 turns; if the validator is doing its job, world facts should never contradict regardless of LLM output.

**Stage 4 (optional) — Player-type adaptation as a multiplier, kept observable.** Only after Stages 1–3 feel good, add PaSSAGE-style weighting of *which* storylet variant to select, driven by *observed behavior* (not a hidden vector), and expect gains concentrated among less-experienced players.

**Cross-cutting:** prefer **accommodation over intervention** everywhere — when a player disrupts a planned arc, re-plan around them rather than silently failing their action; and keep the *causal anchor* inspectable, because legibility of the "because" is what separates your provenance-first director from every trust-breaking director in the record.

## Caveats
- **Some primary numbers come from secondary or community sources.** RimWorld's raid cadence and wealth/combat-point figures are quoted from the community RimWorld Wiki (Cassandra Classic and Wealth pages), not a developer spec; they are well-corroborated but unofficial. L4D's intensity mechanics are from Booth's GDC 2009 deck plus community reconstructions.
- **The 2026 LLM papers cite near-future model names** (GPT-5.2, DeepSeek-V3.2, etc.) consistent with their stated dates; the *architectural* claims (validated writes, constraint-satisfaction framing) are robust regardless of which model is tested, but the *specific percentages* should be read as of those model generations. Orchestrated Reality is explicitly work-in-progress with **no effect sizes** — cite its architecture, not results.
- **PaSSAGE's positive result is segment-limited** (inexperienced players who found the game legible) and did not uniformly beat hand-authored stories; don't over-read it as a general win for player modeling.
- **"Provenance fixes blandness" is a design hypothesis strongly supported by analogy** (Shadows of Doubt, Wildermyth, KoDP) but not by a controlled study on radiant quests specifically; Shadows of Doubt also shows provenance alone doesn't prevent *repetition* fatigue if your situation vocabulary is small.
- **The trust literature is partly about *perception*, not correctness** (Sid Meier): a director can be statistically fair and still feel unfair. Budget for tuning to player *expectation*, not just to correct probability.
