# Academic Social-Simulation Engines Beyond The Sims: A Precedent Study for Chronicle

## TL;DR
- The cluster is real, coherent, and a genuinely closer precedent than The Sims: **Comme il Faut (CiF)/Prom Week**, its open-source successor **Ensemble**, **Versu**, and **City of Gangsters** all implement third-party social inference (an uninvolved character reasoning over facts they were not directly told) — exactly Chronicle's distinguishing goal — whereas The Sims 3 is dyadic-only.
- For a line-by-line source study on the model of IBSEN/HAMLET, the two highest-value targets are **City of Gangsters' BotL** (full C# logic-programming engine, source-available, the only one proven to scale symbolic social inference to a network of roughly 1,200 NPCs) and the **CiF/Prom Week ActionScript source** (the canonical ~5,000-rule social-physics engine, source present but unlicensed). **Ensemble** (JavaScript, BSD-4-clause, but explicitly unmaintained) and the **Praxish/RePraxis** MIT reconstructions of Versu are readable, cleaner secondary reads.
- **Versu itself is citable-but-not-inspectable** — the original engine is proprietary and lost inside Linden Lab; only academic rational-reconstructions of its Praxis logic exist. It nonetheless offers the single most relevant architectural idea for Chronicle's grudges/obligations: a **deontic (permitted/obligated/forbidden) model driven by social practices**.

## Key Findings

**1. All five share the property Chronicle actually cares about — n-ary/third-party social reasoning — but achieve it very differently.** CiF and Ensemble use weighted rule-bases ("influence rules" summed into "volitions"); Versu uses a modal exclusion logic with utility-based action selection over social practices; City of Gangsters uses first-order Horn-clause logic programming over a relationship graph. The Sims 3, by the CiF authors' own comparison, "can only reference the two characters in an interaction," while CiF provides "a level of complexity similar to first order logic in that parties outside of the social exchange can be referenced."

**2. Only City of Gangsters demonstrates scale.** CoG runs symbolic social inference over "a network of roughly 1200 NPCs" (Zubek, Horswill, Robison & Viglione, "Social Modeling via Logic Programming in City of Gangsters," AIIDE 2021, Proc. AAAI Conf. AIIDE 17(1):220–226) in a shipped commercial game; CiF/Prom Week and Versu operate on the order of tens of characters. CoG's key architectural trick (BotL) is what lets it avoid the performance cliffs academic symbolic systems hit.

**3. Inspectability is better than expected for CiF and CoG, worse for Versu.** Contrary to the assumption that only a compiled Prom Week binary survives, the ExpressiveIntelligence/PromWeek repo actually contains the ActionScript engine source in a `trunk/` folder (GitHub detects ~93% ActionScript) — but with no license. BotL is a complete C# engine, source-available but with no license file. Versu's real engine is gone; only MIT-licensed reconstructions (Praxish, RePraxis) can be read.

## Details

### 1. Comme il Faut (CiF) — the ~5,000-rule social-physics engine

**Provenance & primary sources.** Joshua McCoy, Mike Treanor, Ben Samuel, Aaron Reed, Michael Mateas, Noah Wardrip-Fruin (UC Santa Cruz, Expressive Intelligence Studio). Canonical citations: McCoy et al., "Comme il Faut: A System for Authoring Playable Social Models," AIIDE 2011; and the definitive journal article, McCoy, Treanor, Samuel, Reed, Mateas, Wardrip-Fruin, "Social Story Worlds With Comme il Faut," IEEE Transactions on Computational Intelligence and AI in Games (TCIAIG), vol. 6 no. 2, 2014.

**Rule representation.** CiF's atomic unit is the **influence rule**, expressed as Horn clauses (conjunctive predicates with an implication) — deliberately conjunctive-only, mirroring Prolog, so evaluation stays deterministic and avoids a large non-deterministic search space. Predicates test the social state: traits (permanent, e.g. `trait(brainy,x)`), relationships (bidirectional/public binary, e.g. `relationship(dating,x,y)`), statuses (temporary, optionally directional, e.g. `status(hasACrushOn,x,y)`), social networks (directional scalar feelings, e.g. `network(romance,x,y) > 66`), and the Cultural Knowledge Base (props with a "zeitgeist" opinion plus per-character likes/dislikes, queryable for patterns). Rules are grouped into **microtheories** — reusable bundles encoding a social concept (e.g. a "friends" microtheory) — that only contribute their weight when their conditions hold in the current context.

**How an interaction is selected/scored.** CiF loops through: (1) **desire/volition formation** — every character scores volition to play every **social exchange** with every other character by summing the weights of true influence rules; (2) **social exchange selection** — the game (or player) picks one; (3) **social game play** — the responder accepts if the sum of its true responder-rules is ≥ 0, else rejects; (4) **effect selection** — among effects whose conditions hold, the *most salient* (the true condition rule with the most predicates) is chosen; (5) **performance realization** — a template-based NLG "instantiation" renders dialogue/animation; (6) an entry is written into the **Social Facts Database (SFDB)**; (7) **trigger rules** run over the new state, deriving downstream state. Every social exchange has an initiator (i), responder (r), and an optional third party ("other," o).

**How facts propagate to uninvolved characters.** Two mechanisms: the **SFDB**, which stores all played exchanges and referenced items as public social history any character can query ("Naomi will be more interested in dating Simon if Simon has done several nice things to her recently"); and **trigger rules**, which derive new state from configurations not directly asserted. The canonical third-party-inference example: a character gets the `cheating` status when they start a relationship while already dating someone — "x is cheating on y if x and y are dating and there is a character z also dating x." This is precisely the "uninvolved character infers X is cheating on Y" capability.

**Availability & shipped status.** The CiF engine was written in ActionScript 3, developed in tandem with Prom Week. The ExpressiveIntelligence/PromWeek repo contains the ActionScript source in a `trunk/` folder (GitHub detects ~93% ActionScript) plus the compiled `PromWeek.swf` — but carries **no license** (all-rights-reserved by default), and is a single-commit archival dump last touched in 2019.

### 2. Prom Week — the shipped game on CiF

Prom Week is a complete, shipped, critically recognized game: released free on Facebook and Kongregate on Valentine's Day 2012, a 2012 IGF Technical Excellence finalist and 2012 IndieCade finalist. Per the UC Santa Cruz release ("Prom Week Breaks New Ground in Computer Game Design"), the game "uses a sophisticated artificial intelligence system to enable players to shape the social lives of a group of 18 high school students," based on "a set of 5,000 social considerations or 'rules.'" Goals function as "social puzzles" across multiple multi-level "Stories." Its content scope, per lead writer Aaron Reed (aaronareed.net): "Driven by over 5,000 social considerations and 900 'story instantiations,' Prom Week allows for emergent behavior in a highly authored narrative space. Aaron served as Lead Writer on Prom Week, supervising a writing team that produced six thousand lines of dialogue and nearly two thousand character state changes."

**Shipped-vs-theoretical.** Prom Week ships essentially the full CiF engine — CiF is not a stripped subset behind the game; the game *is* the demonstration vehicle for the research engine. The main practical limits are the fixed cast/levels and the compiled-Flash delivery (the Facebook/Kongregate versions are defunct; UCSC now offers only a small embedded SWF and a downloadable SWF). Source: as above, the ActionScript is in the repo `trunk/`, unlicensed.

*(Note on content-count drift: the 2011 FDG paper "Prom Week: Social Physics as Gameplay" cites "over 3,500 sociocultural considerations"; the 5,000 figure is the later, widely-cited shipped-game number. The difference reflects content added between the paper and final release.)*

### 3. Ensemble — CiF's open-source successor

**Confirmed real, not a naming coincidence.** Samuel, Reed, Maddaloni, Mateas, Wardrip-Fruin, "The Ensemble Engine: Next-Generation Social Physics," FDG 2015, which presents "the Ensemble Engine (EE), the first publicly available 'social physics' engine ... inspired by the lessons learned from more than five years building the Comme il Faut (CiF) social physics engine." It is a ground-up JavaScript rewrite of CiF. Repository: **github.com/ensemble-engine/ensemble**, licensed **BSD-4-clause**, ~61 stars, ~350 commits, with a bundled desktop authoring tool and a `loversAndRivals` example.

**Architecture (how it differs from CiF).** Ensemble keeps CiF's core loop (agents recompute volitions each step; an action is chosen; the social record updates; trigger rules fire further state) but generalizes it. Its major change is a **data-driven schema**: rather than hard-coding traits/statuses/relationships, an author declares categories by their *properties* (`isBoolean`, `directionType` = directed/undirected/reciprocal, `duration`, `min/maxValue`, `actionable`). State lives in a **Social Record**. It separates **volition rules** (adjust desire to act) from **trigger rules** (directly change state). Any single predicate involves at most two roles (first/second), but multi-character situations are expressed via combinations of predicates ("the enemy of my enemy is my friend" = three enemy/not-enemy predicates over X, Y, Z) — so, like CiF, third-party inference is compositional rather than built into a single predicate.

**Maintenance verdict.** Genuinely usable and cleanly documented, but **effectively abandoned**: an independent 2024 FDG postmortem ("Paradise," Kreminski et al.) states flatly that "Ensemble has changed hands several times and is no longer maintained," while noting the documentation is thorough and the codebase "reasonably painless" to learn but hard to build a working simulation in.

### 4. Versu — social practices + deontic model

**Primary source (beyond talks).** Richard Evans and Emily Short, "Versu—A Simulationist Storytelling System," IEEE TCIAIG vol. 6 no. 2, 2014, pp. 113–130 (received 2012, published 2013–14) — a full, citable, peer-reviewed architecture paper. Per the IEEE Computational Intelligence Society's list of past recipients, this paper received the TCIAIG Outstanding Paper Award (for work published in 2014). Supporting: Evans, "Introducing Exclusion Logic as a Deontic Logic," DEON 2010.

**Architecture (how it differs from CiF).** Versu is built from two object types: **agents** and **social practices**. A social practice is a reactive joint plan describing a recurring situation (a meal, a conversation, a game, a family) that coordinates agents by the **roles** they play and offers **affordances** (suggested actions) — but never controls agents directly. Crucially, **multiple practices run concurrently**, and an agent's option set is the *union* of affordances from all practices it participates in. Each agent then makes an autonomous choice via **utility-based reactive action selection** ("strong autonomy," versus Façade's centralized drama manager — the DM in Versu is itself just a special practice). The entire world state is a set of sentences in **exclusion logic** (a modal logic; literals form trees/tries, e.g. `brown.relationship.lucy.evaluation.attractive!40`), authored in a DSL called **Praxis**. The whole state is open to inspection/query — a deliberate debuggability design.

**The deontic angle relevant to Chronicle.** Evans's exclusion logic is explicitly a *deontic* logic — it models permitted/obligated/forbidden status. Because practices attach obligations and permissions to roles, a single incoming fact can reconfigure the deontic status of several characters at once (a rude remark opens "disapprove/forgive" affordances for bystanders; a spilled soup creates a responding practice). This "one fact shifts many characters' obligations simultaneously" behavior is the closest published precedent to Chronicle's grudges/obligations writing back into behavior.

**Availability.** The real Versu is **proprietary and effectively lost**: developed at Little Text People, acquired by Linden Lab in 2012, cancelled in early 2014; Emily Short tried to reclaim the IP; per Aaron Reed (2021), "Versu games are no longer playable, except on aging iPads." No engine source was ever open-sourced. What *is* inspectable are academic rational-reconstructions of Praxis: **mkremins/praxish** (JavaScript, MIT license via LICENSE.txt, active, backed by Dameris, Hernandez Roman & Kreminski, "Praxish," AIIDE 2023) and **ShiJbey/RePraxis** (C#, MIT license via LICENSE.md, actively released — v1.4.0 Nov 2024 — with a companion Unity package).

### 5. City of Gangsters — logic programming at ~1,200-NPC scale

**Primary sources.** Two AIIDE 2021 papers: Zubek, Horswill, Robison, Viglione, "Social Modeling via Logic Programming in City of Gangsters" (the technical paper, pp. 220–226); and Robison, Viglione, Zubek, Horswill, "AI Design Lessons for Social Modeling at Scale" (the design paper, pp. 213–219). Affiliations: Robert Zubek and Matthew Viglione (SomaSim), Ian Horswill (Northwestern University), Ethan Robison (Naughty Dog). Shipped commercially by SomaSim, Q3 2021.

**Data model.** A labeled directed graph of characters; each edge is a dyadic, unidirectional relationship tuple ⟨x, y, type, valence, history, context⟩. Valence is a scalar (typically [-50, 50]) computed as the *sum* of a list of **history elements**, each a tuple ⟨Δ-valence, actor, target, expiration, text, context⟩. Two flavors: simple "buffs" (just a valence delta + expiry + explanation) and "external action" elements that point at an actor and target. Critically, each history element is **tetradic** — applied to a dyadic relationship but able to reference a third and fourth party — which makes each nominally-dyadic relationship effectively n-ary and time-varying (expiration timestamps mean arity and valence ebb over time).

**Inference mechanism.** Social norms are encoded as first-order Horn-clause rules that scan the relationship graph for link structures spanning arbitrary persons — e.g. "X should retaliate against Y if Y committed a violent action against some family member F of X." The engine periodically runs these as queries ("find all X,Y such that X should retaliate against Y"); matches insert a new (heavily negative) history element on the relevant edge. The documented emergent example: a player extorts a shopkeeper, the news propagates along family links, and a previously-unmet "hooligan brother" forms a one-sided negative relationship and ambushes the player. This is genuine second-order propagation to uninvolved third parties. The design paper deliberately caps propagation at **second-order** (if A acts on B, consider B–C and C–A, but not further along the transitive closure) to keep it legible and performant, and keeps the norm set tiny (the designers suggest "more than twenty rules strikes us as too many").

**How it scales without the academic performance cliff — BotL.** The queries run in **BotL**, a logic-programming language designed by Ian Horswill for real-time in-engine execution. BotL's departures from traditional Prolog are exactly the performance story: (1) it shares the host's Common Language Runtime object system and memory manager (no second heap/GC), making compound objects opaque to the unifier (DATALOG-like); (2) **static memory allocation** — the VM keeps its own statically-allocated stacks so that after instantiation, calls run in *constant space* and never trigger garbage collection (GC causes dropped frames); (3) a hybrid logic/functional bytecode VM based on the Vienna Abstract Machine (VAM2P), running caller push-opcodes in lockstep with callee match-opcodes for early-out pattern matching. The result, per the authors, is that "a declarative language could be performant at scale in a commercial game" — the only system in this cluster proven to scale symbolic social inference to ~1,200 agents. (Their one documented failure: using the CatSAT constraint solver to generate the entire ~1,200-person family tree at once was too slow, so they fell back to forward-simulation over generations.)

**Availability.** BotL is at **github.com/ianhorswill/BotL** — full C# engine source, ~123 commits, but **no license file** (source-available, all-rights-reserved). Horswill himself now says he wouldn't necessarily recommend it, pointing to his newer TED/TELL languages. A demo of the CoG social-inference system, **MicroCoG**, is referenced on Horswill's site (github.com/ianhorswill/MicroCoG) but its license could not be independently confirmed. City of Gangsters itself is a closed-source commercial product.

### How each differs from Chronicle's existing belief/rumor/grudge design

- **Belief provenance:** *None of the five track per-belief provenance the way Chronicle does.* CiF's SFDB and CoG's history-element `text` strings record *what happened* and can explain a relationship's current value (CoG's UI even lists the contributing events), but these are objective public social facts, not per-NPC subjective beliefs with a source/chain-of-custody. Versu comes closest philosophically: beliefs are sentences scoped inside an agent (`mr_collins.beliefs.clergymen_should_marry`) and the inspector can find "what is causing a fact to become true" — but this is authoring-time debuggability, not in-fiction provenance that NPCs reason about.
- **Rumor mutation in transit:** *Absent in all five.* CoG propagates *news* along the social graph (extortion reaches family members) and CiF/Versu spread social facts, but the propagated fact is faithful — it does not mutate as it spreads. Chronicle's mutating-rumor mechanic has no direct analog here; the nearest research pointer is Kreminski's separate 2023 gossip-simulation work, not these engines.
- **Grudges/obligations writing back into behavior:** *This is the cluster's strongest overlap.* CoG grudges are first-class (negative history elements that gate action space and trigger ambushes); CiF statuses like `angryAt` feed back into volition scoring; Versu's deontic practices are the richest model — obligations/permissions attached to roles directly determine each agent's available actions, and one triggering fact can shift many agents' deontic status at once.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Read now, for the scaling architecture (highest priority): City of Gangsters' BotL.** Chronicle targets a whole-world Skyrim population, so the one system in this cluster that solved symbolic social inference at ~1,200-NPC scale is the most directly load-bearing. Read the AIIDE 2021 technical paper first, then read the BotL C# source at github.com/ianhorswill/BotL — specifically the VM (static allocation, VAM2P lockstep matching) and the tetradic history-element model. **Benchmark that would change this:** if Chronicle's belief store is designed as an append-only per-NPC event log rather than a queried graph, BotL's query-centric model is less directly portable and CiF becomes the better read.

**Stage 2 — Read for the canonical rule/volition model: CiF ActionScript source.** Read the TCIAIG "Social Story Worlds" paper as the map, then the `trunk/` ActionScript in ExpressiveIntelligence/PromWeek for the concrete implementation of microtheories, influence-rule summation, SFDB queries, and trigger rules (the cheating-inference example). **Caveat/threshold:** the repo is unlicensed — treat it as reference-only for understanding, not as code to copy into Chronicle. If legal cleanliness matters, substitute Ensemble (BSD-4-clause) as the readable stand-in for the same architecture.

**Stage 3 — Read for the deontic obligations model: Praxish/RePraxis (as proxies for Versu).** Since the real Versu is unreadable, read the Evans & Short TCIAIG paper for the social-practice/deontic design, then RePraxis (C#, MIT, maintained) or Praxish (JS, MIT) to see exclusion logic implemented. This is the single best source for Chronicle's "one fact shifts many NPCs' obligations" mechanic. **Threshold:** only invest deeply here if Chronicle intends obligations to be a first-class deontic layer (permitted/obligated/forbidden) rather than scalar grudge values.

**Stage 4 — Skim only: Ensemble.** Useful as a clean, BSD-licensed, well-documented reference implementation of the CiF loop in JavaScript, but its abandoned status and documented difficulty building working simulations mean it is not worth a deep study unless you specifically want a permissively-licensed starting codebase.

## Verdict: inspectable vs. citable-only

**Worth a deep line-by-line source study (real code exists to read):**
- **City of Gangsters / BotL** — full C# engine, source-available (no license: read, don't copy). The unique scaling precedent. **Top pick.**
- **CiF / Prom Week** — ActionScript engine source present in-repo (no license). The canonical social-physics rule engine. **Second pick.**
- **Ensemble** — JavaScript, BSD-4-clause, most legally reusable and best-documented, but unmaintained. **Best-licensed read, secondary priority.**
- **Praxish / RePraxis** — MIT-licensed, maintained reconstructions of Versu's logic layer; inspectable proxies for an otherwise-lost engine.

**Citable-but-not-inspectable (papers/talks only, no real engine code):**
- **Versu** — proprietary, cancelled by Linden Lab, no source ever released, games no longer playable. Cite the IEEE TCIAIG 2014 paper and Evans's DEON 2010 exclusion-logic paper; study its ideas through the Praxish/RePraxis reconstructions.

## Caveats
- **Content-count drift for Prom Week/CiF:** the "~5,000 rules" figure is the post-release/journal number; the 2011 FDG paper says "over 3,500 sociocultural considerations." Both are correct for their moment; cite ~5,000 for the shipped game, ~3,500 for the 2011 snapshot.
- **"Unlicensed" ≠ open source:** both the CiF ActionScript and BotL are *source-available* (readable on GitHub) but carry no license, so default all-rights-reserved copyright applies. They are safe to *read and learn from* but not to copy into Chronicle. Only Ensemble (BSD-4-clause) and the Praxis reconstructions (MIT) are cleanly reusable.
- **MicroCoG unverified:** the City of Gangsters social-inference demo repo is referenced by Horswill but its license/contents were not independently confirmed; check github.com/ianhorswill/MicroCoG directly before relying on it.
- **None of the five is a drop-in match for Chronicle's headline features:** per-belief provenance and in-transit rumor mutation are genuinely novel to Chronicle relative to this cluster; the overlap is strongest on grudges/obligations (all five) and third-party inference (all five). Treat these engines as architectural precedent for the *inference substrate*, not for the provenance/rumor layer.
- **CoG's second-order cap is a design choice, not a limit:** the authors deliberately restrict propagation to second-order for legibility/performance; Chronicle may want deeper propagation, which reintroduces the scaling questions BotL was built to answer.
