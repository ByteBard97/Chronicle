# Design Lessons from Crusader Kings-Style Social Simulation: A Postmortem for Skyrim NPC Reactivity

## TL;DR
- **Copy CK's load-bearing core**: unilateral, fully transparent opinion values summed from small named modifiers that *drive the AI directly*, wired to visible personality traits — this is the single mechanic Paradox and academics agree makes characters feel "real," and it survived from CK2's first build to CK3 essentially unchanged.
- **Avoid CK's documented failure modes**: "opinion-modifier soup," decay-gaming, the "everyone plots murder constantly" problem, and — most important for you — *legibility failures* where players couldn't understand why a character acted (the canonical complaint being a full-love vassal joining a faction to overthrow you, with no warning).
- **Adapt, don't copy, the delivery layer**: CK3's move from mean-time-to-happen (MTTH) random events to `on_action` hooks, its stress/dread/hooks systems, and its "tooltips-in-tooltips" legibility scaffolding are the mature answers to problems CK2 shipped with — but Paradox never fully solved AI legibility or the tension between "predictable/legible" and "surprising/emergent."

## Key Findings

1. **The opinion system is the keystone, and its design philosophy is explicit.** CK2 lead designer Henrik Fåhraeus decided early that state-to-state relation modeling "would not suffice" for a character game; he built one unilateral opinion value (−100 to +100) "summed up from a number of clear reasons why someone would like or dislike you," with the AI relying "heavily on the opinion values, meaning it can act intelligently and in character." Crucially he reports the system "never really got much criticism and did not undergo any major revisions from the first implementation." This is your model: subjective, per-character, transparent, and *action-driving*.

2. **"Character-first" is a stated, deliberate philosophy — and it has a cost.** CK3's Dev Diary #0 lists "Character Focus" as goal #1: "Crusader Kings is clearly and unequivocally about individual characters... This makes CK most suited for memorable emergent stories, and we wanted to bring characters into all important gameplay mechanics." But CK3 also prioritized "Player Freedom and Progression" ("cater to all player fantasies"), and a significant part of the community argues these two goals are in tension — that making systems more predictable and player-serving weakened the story-generation engine.

3. **Legibility was CK2's biggest recurring failure and CK3's biggest deliberate fix.** The canonical complaint is variations of "why did my vassal revolt / why did a full-love vassal join a rebellion?" CK3 responded with (a) tooltips-in-tooltips so any number can be drilled into, (b) exposing `ai_value_modifiers` so NPC choices trace to traits, and (c) new systems (dread, hooks, stress) that make *why* a character can/can't act against you explicit. This is the richest vein of lessons for your mod.

4. **The event architecture is the "social actions" layer, and Paradox changed how it fires.** CK2 leaned on MTTH (mean-time-to-happen) probability; CK3 abandoned MTTH entirely for `on_action` hooks because MTTH "caused a lot of weird statistical anomalies due to working off of pure probabilities, and absolutely tanked performance," and became "more of a disadvantage than anything else" when balancing large numbers of events. This is a direct, hard-won engineering lesson.

5. **Genre neighbors reveal the design space — and the same legibility trap.** Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord's relation/loyalty system is widely criticized for opaque, punishing, hard-to-reverse penalties; Total War: Three Kingdoms' "Guanxi"/Satisfaction system is the closest analog to CK (characters leave your faction if dissatisfied) but is criticized as micromanagement; Wildermyth and King of Dragon Pass keep NPC-initiated action *legible* by radically constraining scope (few characters, hand-authored vignettes, advisors who literally speak their agenda).

## Details

### (1) Paradox postmortems & the "character-first" philosophy

**The founding design decision (primary source: Rowan Kaiser, "The Surprising Design of Crusader Kings II," Gamasutra/Game Developer, 2013 — the interview in which Fåhraeus lays out the opinion system; Kaiser summarizes CK2 as a game that "successfully models historical human behavior using a transparent system," and this article is cited in the Lucat & Haahr academic bibliography).** Fåhraeus: "Very early on during the design phase of Crusader Kings II, I realized that the way we usually model relations between states in our games would not suffice." His solution: "a simple, unified mechanic; easy to understand, yet deep and meaningful. Thus, we came up with the Opinions of Crusader Kings II; one single value summed up from a number of clear reasons why someone would like or dislike you (e.g. holding desired titles, refusing a request, or just plain old personality chemistry.) Opinions are unilateral, meaning the feeling is not necessarily mutual. The AI, of course, relies heavily on the opinion values, meaning it can act intelligently and in character."

Three design principles are embedded here that you should treat as the core lesson:
- **Transparency is a feature, not a compromise.** "All these numbers are visible and easy to access via tooltips."
- **The value must *drive AI behavior*, not just gate player actions.** This is what separates a reputation *number* from social *reactivity*.
- **Reasonable surprise, not weird surprise.** Fåhraeus: "There is... a huge difference between unexpected (weird) and expected (reasonable) random outcomes. The former is normally undesired... whereas the latter is exactly what we are aiming for." His stated goal was that characters do things "for mostly logical and predictable reasons."

**AI characters need "clear opinions they could act upon."** Fåhraeus framed the whole game around real medieval intrigue: he cites Conan II, Duke of Brittany, who died 11 December 1066 at the siege of Château-Gontier — per the historical account, "he was found dead after donning poisoned riding gloves," with Duke William "widely suspected." Fåhraeus's takeaway: "In order to facilitate this, AI characters especially needed to have clear opinions they could act upon."

**The GDC 2014 talk ("Emergent Stories in Crusader Kings II") is the definitive designer statement.** Fåhraeus's slide deck lays out his "recipe for emergent drama": *open-ended gameplay; a great many AI actors; AI personalities and opinions; changing conditions; conflict; low morals — add salt, sprinkle with scripted narrative, and stir.* His four narrative forms: Linear (written), Branching (written), Improvised, Emergent — with CK and The Sims as the emergent exemplars. His "story engine" ingredients: Quantity of Agents, Quality of Agents, Limited Resources, Changing Conditions, Multiple Courses of Action, Repercussions, Dubious Morality. Key takeaways from the slides: "AI actors are crucial" and "Scripted and emergent narrative make great friends." Notably his stated *future* directions — "Emergence Detection" (AI scanning for story patterns to emphasize with music/animation), "Chronicling," and a "Reactive World" — are things CK2 had *not* yet done, i.e. open problems, not shipped features.

**CK2→CK3: what they deliberately changed and why.**
- **Stress (new in CK3).** Per Dev Diary #31, stress is "a representation of a character's mental well-being" that accumulates primarily "when the demands of the realm force them to take actions which go against their nature." This is the mechanical enforcement of trait-consistency: it makes personality *cost something* to violate. Fåhraeus/Oltner's intent was to deepen roleplay. (Community critique: at launch stress could be min-maxed into a currency, and "stress cascades" could wipe out families — a balance failure Paradox patched repeatedly, including a Dec 2023 change reducing stroke risk and adding an opt-out game rule.)
- **Dread (new in CK3).** Fåhraeus at PDXCon 2019: "That's one of the things that CK2, I felt, was missing. Your vassals had to love you for you to rule viably, and we want to cater to the player fantasy of being King Joffrey or the Bastard of Bolton. So you can build up a value we call 'dread' which makes your vassals afraid to act against you." Mechanically, dread creates Intimidated/Terrified states: a Terrified vassal *cannot* join a faction or scheme against you — *unless* a faction is already gaining discontent and you're outnumbered. This is a clean legibility win: it converts "will they act against me?" into an inspectable state.
- **Schemes/Secrets/Hooks (CK3) replace CK2 plots.** Per Dev Diary #5, murder plots became "schemes" with agents, a success chance (scheme progresses ~10 steps then attempts to execute), and a secrecy check; discovered secrets become "hooks" (leverage) that let you force actions like vassal-contract changes *without* incurring tyranny. This restructures intrigue from a shared opinion-based plot pool into targeted, individually-legible schemes.
- **Systems dropped as failures.** Per a PC PowerPlay (Jan 2020) preview, lead designer Alexander Oltner "admits [the CK2 inventory system] was horrible for balance and led to stat bloat," and it was "tossed out"; Fåhraeus's stated goal was "Rather than trying to match the width of Crusader Kings II... we went deep instead." CK3's Dev Diary #0 codifies this as "go deep rather than wide."

**The core controversy you should design around.** A widely-referenced community/GDC-informed argument holds that CK2 "accidentally built a great emergent story machine," and that CK3, by making "game systems more predictable and catering to player fantasies," worked "at odds with building a great story generator." Whether or not you accept the verdict, the *tension is real and unsolved*: legibility and player-empowerment pull against surprise and emergent drama. Your design has to pick a point on that spectrum consciously.

### (2) Known failure modes (with the emphasis on legibility you asked for)

- **Opinion-modifier soup.** CK2's opinion is summed from a large, open-ended list of timed and situational modifiers. Fåhraeus himself: "I don't think I anticipated the sheer number of opinion reasons we ended up with." The upside is richness; the downside is that late-game a character's opinion becomes a wall of ±small numbers that's hard to reason about at a glance. **Lesson: cap the number of *simultaneously salient* modifiers, or surface only the top 3–4 drivers plus a "more" affordance.**

- **The "everyone plots murder constantly" problem.** In CK2, "Possessed and Lunatic characters often plot," and players complained the world felt like an idiot-plot murderfest ("how come a guy with 100 opinion of me joined a plot to kill me?"). CK3's dread/scheme redesign was partly a response — narrowing *who* will act hostile and making it inspectable.

- **Players gaming decay / bribes.** Because opinion modifiers are timed and gifts raise opinion, players learned to game the system (send-gift loops; "auto-end plot" to suppress spam). A CK3 flavor mod author notes vanilla send-gift math was so exploitable that "gifting 50 gold gives more than 100 opinion... Greedy emperors are easily bribed." **Lesson: make the highest-leverage social levers either bounded, diminishing, or personality-gated.**

- **Legibility failures — the central case.** The signature complaint is the full-love vassal who still betrays you. Verbatim from a CK2 Steam thread ("Why has a vassal with 100 opinion joined a faction to replace me?", user laagamer, May 17 2020): *"my deceased father's BEST FRIEND, who I can't stress enough has 100 opinion of me, has screwed my inheritance... What the hell am I supposed to do if people that LITERALLY CAN'T LIKE ME ANY BETTER join factions to overthrow me?"* The community-surfaced rule in CK3 is that *high-opinion vassals don't start factions, but will join an existing faction under conditions* (e.g., claimant factions on succession, or when the liege is weak) — a rule that is **true but not surfaced in the moment**, which is exactly why it reads as a bug to players. Related: "GIVE US NOTIFICATIONS FOR REBELLIONS THAT ARE ABOUT TO HAPPEN," and AI vassals modifying feudal contracts to force themselves onto the council with the player only notified after the fact. **The meta-lesson for your mod: an NPC-initiated hostile action must arrive with (a) advance warning, (b) an inspectable causal chain, and (c) a counterplay window — or players will read your systemic drama as randomness or bugs.**

- **Event repetition / "more events is not the answer."** CK2's Reaper's Due shipped with events firing 7–8 times in quick succession (a genuine bug), and CK3 players argue that piling on random flavor events ("the cat story") doesn't create emergent narrative: "Emergent gameplay and stories come from unforeseen interactions and dilemmas brought about by the procedure of the gameplay accumulating over time... not modifiers bought through a decision offered in an artificial event." **Lesson: state-change-driven consequences > volume of authored random events.**

- **Trait/consequence disconnect.** CK3 players complain "traits doesn't affect your event options" (a Lunatic can pick rational options; a Cruel character can act Compassionate free of friction). Stress partially addresses this by *pricing* off-personality choices rather than *forbidding* them. **Lesson: enforce personality through cost/friction, not hard locks — but make the cost visible.**

### (3) Event/decision architecture as the "social actions" layer

**How CK events work (official CK3 wiki + Dev Diary #30).** A CK3 event has: a namespace + unique ID; a `trigger` block (conditions gating eligibility); an `immediate` block (effects run before display); `option` blocks (choices, each with effects, an `ai_chance` weight, and a `stress_impact`); and theming/portraits. Events fire either from a specific world action or on a regular pulse, and can be placed in weighted lists. In CK3, unlike older games, "events do not fire automatically" — they must be fired from an on_action, decision, character interaction, or story cycle.

**The MTTH → on_action shift is the key authoring lesson.** From Dev Diary #30 (CK3): in CK2, events were often driven by Mean Time To Happen. But "when balancing extremely large numbers of events against each other, the flexibility of this system becomes more of a disadvantage than anything else, making it difficult to govern how frequently an event should spawn without particularly stringent triggers. It also caused a lot of weird statistical anomalies due to working off of pure probabilities, and absolutely tanked performance." CK3 uses "entirely on_actions to fire events" — hooks attached "either when a specific action occurs in the world (e.g., when a character is born) or on a regular pulse (e.g., every five years)." **For a Skyrim mod (Papyrus/SKSE), this maps directly: prefer event-driven triggers (OnHit, OnActivate, faction-state changes, quest stages) and bounded polling pulses over per-actor random rolls — both for legibility and for performance, which is a real constraint in a Bethesda engine.**

**Authoring at scale — the maintainability tooling.** Dev Diary #30 details the techniques Paradox built to author thousands of events without chaos: `scripted_triggers` (write a complex condition set once, reference everywhere, fix in one place — "Instant maintenance savings, immediate huge reductions in bug potential!"); inline scripted triggers; `first_valid` blocks for context-sensitive flavor text (so one event serves French/Ashari/elderly variants); scripted lists (`ordered_in_list`, `random_in_list`) to select characters ("grab every ornery old man among my vassals, pick the two angriest, have them argue"). **Lesson: invest early in reusable condition/selection primitives; the cost of authoring social content is dominated by maintenance, not first-write.**

**What makes an event feel like agency vs. dice.** The `ai_chance` block plus exposed `ai_value_modifiers` are how CK3 ensures "characters act in accordance with their personality as much as possible by weighing almost all event options up or down based on appropriate ai_value_modifiers." **The felt difference between "a character did this" and "the dice did this" is whether the outcome traces back to that character's visible traits/opinions/state — which is a legibility property, not a randomness property.**

### (4) Genre neighbors: the same problem, different scope choices

- **Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord (relations + fief loyalty).** Relations are numeric per-lord and per-notable; fief loyalty drives rebellions. The documented failure is opacity + punishment asymmetry: players report losing relation "for seemingly no good reason," penalties that are trivial to incur and enormously slow to repair, and a −3 same-culture loyalty penalty so punishing that community advice is literally to install a random same-culture governor from a tavern. Executing a lord tanks relations with *every* faction and spams notifications. **Lesson (what to avoid): NPC-initiated consequences that are easy to trigger, hard to understand, and near-impossible to reverse feel like bugs, not drama.**

- **Total War: Three Kingdoms (Guanxi + Satisfaction) — the closest CK analog.** Creative Assembly modeled character relationships on the Chinese concept of *guanxi*: characters form positive/negative opinions from shared events, battle record, faction friendliness, and trait compatibility, and — critically — a dissatisfied character "will automatically leave the faction to seek employment elsewhere and bring their own retinues." Traits carry opinion hooks (a "formidable" character "Supports War" and bonds with warlike leaders; an "ambitious" character gains income but *loses satisfaction* and is more likely to defect). This is state-driven NPC-initiated action almost identical in spirit to CK. Community critique: satisfaction becomes a money-sink micromanagement treadmill. **Lesson: the "unhappy character leaves/defects" action is powerful and legible *if* the drivers are few and inspectable; it becomes a chore if you must constantly top up a decaying meter.**

- **Wildermyth (relationship arcs).** Characters form Friendship/Rivalry/Romance (five levels each) via shared combat, shared tasks, and events, gated by personality-type compatibility (e.g., a "bookish" hero likes bookish/healer/snark, dislikes greedy/goofball). Relationships have mechanical *and* narrative payoffs (Lover's Vengeance damage buff). Design writing (Cannibal Halfling's GDC-vault piece) distills the takeaways: "Make the introduction easy... Make sure the most interesting events in your characters' lives happen in play. Make sure your game has stakes... make sure that your characters impact the world, and that the impact is seen." **Lesson: legibility comes from *few relationship types with clear names and clear consequences*, not from a continuous opinion scalar.**

- **King of Dragon Pass / Six Ages (advisor ring).** The clan "ring" of advisors is present on every screen; click one and they speak advice "based on their skills, their position, and their patron god," and "they never, ever agree" — advisors sometimes give advice that "serves their own agendas better than it serves the clan." The Six Ages dev blog shows how carefully they gate *which advisor speaks* to keep it in-character (e.g., suppressing an advisor commenting rudely about their own mother). **Lesson: the most legible possible form of "subjective belief → NPC-initiated action" is an NPC who literally tells you their biased opinion in their own voice, at the moment it's relevant. For a Skyrim mod, surfacing a grudge/obligation as spoken dialogue or a journal line is far more legible than a hidden scalar.**

### (5) Academic analysis: what makes characters feel "real"

The authoritative academic treatment is **Bertrand Lucat & Mads Haahr, "What Makes a Successful Emergent Narrative: The Case of Crusader Kings II," ICIDS 2015 (Interactive Storytelling), LNCS vol. 9445, Springer, pp. 259–266, DOI 10.1007/978-3-319-27036-4_25** (both authors at Trinity College Dublin).

Their central thesis (verbatim abstract): CK2 succeeds "by deploying hybrid narrative techniques that rely as much on the systems of the simulation as they do upon the actions of ambitious autonomous agents and scripted vignettes." In other words, *character realism is a three-way product*: (a) simulation systems, (b) autonomous agents with their own motivations, and (c) authored set-piece vignettes.

They identify **three narrative layers on a "continual sliding scale of detail":**
1. **Grand Strategy** — military/economy/religion/territory management, "least detailed... most typical of the grand strategy genre."
2. **Feudal Politics** — vassals' "ambitions and desire for power... always creates an unstable matrix of relations that can easily be upset by interpersonal conflicts."
3. **Dynasty Politics** — marriage, children, inheritance; "the layer in which most attention is given to the traits that inform the... opinion system," where "interpersonal relations go beyond the purely political to enter into more intimate dynamics."

Mechanisms they credit as load-bearing:
- **The opinion system** ("primarily driving two of these narrative layers"), explicitly traced to *The Sims'* relationship modeling.
- **Traits carrying opinion modifiers as the engine of conflict.** Verbatim: "Every autonomous agent in the game, as well as the player's current character, are characterized by a number of traits which carry opinion modifiers that affect a scale of −100 to 100 in characters' mutual opinions of one another. Complementary and opposing traits create tensions... which in turn drives a great deal of intrigue, betrayal, and conflict." Identity (traits) and reactivity (opinion) are the *same substrate*.
- **Autonomous / "independently motivated" agents**, which they argue (building on Henry Jenkins' "Game Design as Narrative Architecture" and Louchart et al.'s "purposeful authoring for emergent narrative") are essential: "the [role] of independently motivated agents is not to be understated in the way that an emergent narrative... can and should rely upon them to create a narrative that involves the player."

The paper's deepest point for your purposes: CK2 shows "emergent narrative techniques focused on character interaction, as found in dollhouse simulators like The Sims, can be successfully integrated within another genre" — i.e., *the character-reactivity substrate is portable across genres*, which is precisely the bet you're making by porting it into Skyrim.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Build the legible core first (before any content).**
1. Implement a single, unilateral, per-NPC **opinion/standing scalar toward the player (and toward key other NPCs)**, computed as a sum of a *small, named, inspectable* set of modifiers. Follow CK2: transparency is a feature. Every modifier must have human-readable text and a source.
2. Make that scalar **drive NPC-initiated behavior directly** (dialogue tone, refusal of requests, initiating a grudge action), not merely gate player options. This is the difference between a reputation number and social reactivity.
3. Tie modifiers to **visible personality traits** so beliefs and reactivity share one substrate (the Lucat/Haahr "traits carry opinion modifiers" insight, the CK trait system, Wildermyth's compatibility types).

**Stage 2 — Make every NPC-initiated action legible (the thing CK never fully nailed).**
4. For any hostile/obligation-driven action, guarantee the **"three-part legibility contract": advance warning, an inspectable cause chain, and a counterplay window.** Directly answer, in-fiction, "why is this character doing this?" — ideally as *spoken dialogue or a journal entry* (King of Dragon Pass model), not a buried tooltip. This directly forecloses the "full-love vassal betrays me with no warning" failure.
5. Adopt CK3's **dread-style inspectable gate**: expose a clear state ("this NPC fears you / owes you / holds a grudge") that a player can read *before* the action fires, and that determines whether the NPC will act.
6. Surface only the **top 3–4 opinion drivers** at a glance with a drill-down (CK3 tooltips-in-tooltips), to avoid "modifier soup."

**Stage 3 — Wire the "social actions" layer for the Bethesda engine.**
7. Prefer **event-driven hooks and bounded pulses over per-actor random rolls** (CK3's MTTH→on_action lesson). In Papyrus, lean on native events (OnHit/OnActivate/OnDeath, faction and quest-stage changes) and infrequent, staggered polling; avoid per-frame or per-NPC continuous RNG for both legibility and performance.
8. Build **reusable condition and selection primitives early** (CK's scripted_triggers / scripted lists). Author social content as data, not bespoke scripts.
9. Enforce personality through **cost/friction (stress model), not hard locks**, and show the cost.

**Stage 4 — Constrain scope to protect legibility.**
10. Resist "more events is the answer." Prioritize **state-change consequences** over volume of authored flavor. If you must add random events, gate them hard and dedupe (CK2's Reaper's Due repetition bug).
11. Keep the number of relationship *types* small and named (Wildermyth: friend/rival/lover; for you: grudge/obligation/debt), with clear mechanical and narrative payoffs.

**Benchmarks that should change your plan:**
- If playtesters ever ask "why did that NPC do that?" and can't self-answer from in-game information within ~15 seconds → your legibility contract is failing; stop adding content and fix surfacing.
- If players discover a dominant exploit loop (gift-spam, decay-gaming) → bound or personality-gate that lever (CK's bribe/decay lesson).
- If save/reload or repeated events make the world feel random rather than authored → shift from RNG-fire to state-driven fire.
- If NPC-initiated actions feel like a maintenance treadmill (Three Kingdoms satisfaction) → reduce meter decay and increase discrete, event-based triggers.

## Caveats
- **Much of the CK "failure" evidence is community forum/Steam sentiment, not controlled study.** I've flagged where a claim is designer-stated (dev diaries, GDC, named interviews — high confidence) vs. player-reported (forums/Steam — directional but real). The full-love-vassal-betrayal and "why did my vassal revolt" complaints are abundant and consistent (the quoted Steam thread is one representative example), but the *underlying rule* (high-opinion vassals join but don't start factions) is community-surfaced, not always officially documented per-patch.
- **CK2 vs CK3 is partly a values debate, not a settled verdict.** The "CK3 weakened the story engine" argument is a strongly-held community position, but CK3 is also a major commercial success — Paradox announced it had "sold four million copies across platforms" as of 23 April 2025 (up from the 3-million milestone of 1 September 2023) — with higher average concurrency than CK2. "Predictable/legible" vs "emergent/surprising" is a genuine design trade-off, not a solved problem. Take the "what to avoid" items as risks to manage, not laws.
- **The academic paper body was partly retrieved via indexed snippets** (ResearchGate rate-limited full download); the abstract, three-layer framing, the verbatim trait/opinion passage, authorship, venue, and DOI are confirmed, but a few sentences were truncated mid-clause.
- **Engine translation is non-trivial.** CK is a data-driven grand-strategy engine with thousands of simulated actors; Skyrim's Papyrus is comparatively slow and event-oriented with tight save-bloat and performance limits. The *design* lessons port cleanly; the *implementation* will require aggressive scoping (limit the count of fully-simulated reactive NPCs, stagger updates, persist state carefully).
- **Open problems CK never solved** (treat as your research frontier, not copyable answers): automatic "emergence detection" (surfacing the emergent story to the player with music/framing — Fåhraeus's 2014 wishlist item); making AI long-term intent legible ("why is this character being strategic about me?"); and reconciling deep legibility with genuine surprise. These are where a focused mod can actually advance the state of the art.
